The Genesis

The VDP & Planes

The Genesis draws its picture with a chip that owns its own memory and will not let the CPU near it. Everything the machine shows has to squeeze through two 16-bit ports and land in the right table, and for the scrolling planes that table is one 16-bit word per 8x8 cell. This page is that whole story: the ports and the command word, the read-modify-write hazard that turns ordinary 68000 instructions into hangs, the planes and their six legal sizes, the two scroll tables, the one priority bit that produces seven layers, and the Window layer that replaces plane A rather than sitting on top of it.

This page assumes only what The Machine teaches: that the console is several chips sharing one address bus, that the video chip's two ports sit at fixed addresses in the 68000's map, and that the VDP owns three memories of its own. Everything else is built here.

Almost everything on this page is one chip behaving one way, and where it is not the claim carries a small badge. Two of them, because two different things vary and they are easy to confuse: NTSC / PAL means the console's region changes the answer, while V30 mode means a display MODE changes it, chosen by a register bit rather than by which machine you own. The second one carries a restriction of its own, and each badge says so where it appears.

1 · The chip that owns its own memory

The VDP is not a peripheral the 68000 writes pictures into. It is a second processor with three private memories of its own, and it reads them itself, every scanline, to build the picture. The 68000 never sees any of the three. It cannot put an address on the bus and read a byte of video memory the way it reads work RAM, because those memories are not wired to the 68000's address bus at all.

MemorySizeHoldsOne entry is
VRAM65,536 bytesanything the VDP draws from: tile art, the plane maps, the sprite list, the horizontal scroll tablea byte

Only VRAM is general purpose. CRAM and VSRAM are fixed in size and fixed in purpose, and nothing else can be stored in them. VRAM is the one that has to be budgeted, because everything a game shows competes for the same 65,536 bytes: Sega's own inventory of what lives there runs to seven kinds of thing, and the tile art is only one of them. Section 5 spends that budget on a real layout.

New here? · Why can't the CPU just write to video memory?

On a modern machine there is one address space and video memory is part of it. Write to the right address and a pixel changes. That is not how this console is built.

The 68000 has one set of address wires, and everything it can reach hangs off them: the cartridge, work RAM, the controller ports, the video chip's two ports. The video chip's 64KB is on the OTHER side of that chip, wired to nothing but the VDP itself. There is no address the 68000 can name that means "byte 300 of video memory", in the same way there is no phone number that means "the third drawer of my neighbour's desk". You have to ask the neighbour.

So every access is a two-part conversation. First you tell the VDP where you want to go and whether you are reading or writing. Then you push or pull 16 bits at a time through a single doorway. The whole of the next section is the grammar of that conversation, and almost every performance decision a Genesis game makes comes back to the fact that the doorway is narrow and only open wide between frames.

One consequence is worth stating before anything else, because it shapes the rest of the page: the VDP reads its memories on its own schedule, and it does not stop to be polite. While the picture is being drawn the chip is busy fetching tiles and scroll values, so the CPU's accesses have to fit around it. That is why bulk video work happens in VBlank, the short gap between frames when the VDP is not drawing anything.

2 · Two ports and one command word

There are two doorways, both 16 bits wide, both at fixed addresses in the 68000's map.

AddressPortWhat it is for
$C00000data portthe bytes themselves, going in or coming out
$C00004control portwritten: a command word. Read: the VDP's status

Each port answers at a second address as well, $C00002 and $C00006, which Sega documents as functionally equivalent to the first. Charles MacDonald's hardware-tested notes record that the VDP in fact has mirrors right across a much larger window, $C00000 to $DFFFFF, and that reading an address inside that window which does not match the decode pattern locks the machine. That last part is recorded by one source only, so treat it as his measurement rather than as a settled fact, and stay on the four documented addresses.

The command word, form one: set a register

The VDP has 24 write-only configuration registers, numbered 0 to 23, plus one read-only status register. None of them has an address. You set one by writing a single 16-bit word to the CONTROL port, shaped like this:

Put the two layouts next to each other and the thing that separates them becomes visible. The register form always begins 10. The address form begins with CD1 and CD0, the low two bits of the six-bit operation code, and the six documented operations use only three of the four possible pairs there: 00 for the three reads, 01 for a VRAM or VSRAM write, 11 for a CRAM write. 10 is the one combination no documented operation produces, in DMA form or otherwise. So a first word beginning 10 cannot be the start of an address command, and a first word that does not begin 10 cannot be a register write. That is enough to tell the two apart on the first word alone, which is why one form is one word and the other is two. No source states this as a described mechanism; it is what the two published layouts add up to, which is why it is worked out here in the open rather than asserted.

New here? · Why is the address split across two words?

A VRAM address needs 16 bits, and there are 16 bits in the first word, but two of them were already spent saying what kind of access this is. So the low 14 bits of the address ride in the first word and the top two bits are stranded in the second word, in its two lowest positions, as far from the rest of the address as it is possible to get.

Nobody assembles that by hand twice. Every engine writes it once as a helper:

((address & $3FFF) << 16) | ((address & $C000) >> 14) | command

Read it as three deposits into one 32-bit value. The low 14 bits of the address get shifted up into the high word, because the high word is the first word sent. The top two bits get shifted DOWN to the bottom, which is where the second word wants them. The command bits are dropped in last. Send the whole longword to the control port and the 68000's two halves of the write become the VDP's two words, in order.

The operation codes

Six operations are documented, and they are what the CD bits select:

CD0 is the write flag: it is 1 for all three writes and 0 for all three reads. After that the tidiness stops. VSRAM is picked out by CD2, but CRAM READ is picked out by CD3 while CRAM WRITE is picked out by CD1. There is no rule to remember here, and that is the honest lesson: the encoding is irregular enough that every engine wraps it in a macro and never types it again.

Setting CD5 turns the same command into a DMA operation to the same destination, which is why the disassembly comment above calls CD5 "DMA operation". What the DMA modes are, how the length and source registers are loaded, and what each one costs are another page's business; nothing on this page needs them. The third DMA mode, the VRAM-to-VRAM copy, has no row above for a reason: the manual's table does not carry its code, and this page does not assert one. The disassembly comment further up offers CD4 as the VRAM-copy bit, which is one game's annotation rather than a documented code.

Auto-increment, and the size of an access

You do not re-issue the address for every word. Register 15 holds an auto-increment value, a plain number from 0 to 255 that the VDP adds to its internal address after EVERY access, in Sega's words independent of the data size. Set it to 2 and a run of word writes walks forward through memory. Set it to 128 and consecutive writes land 128 bytes apart, which on a 64-cell-wide plane means straight down a column instead of along a row.

Access size is where the manual will actively mislead you. Its printed text says the data port accepts byte writes. Charles MacDonald measured what the hardware does instead: a write to the VDP data or control port is interpreted as a 16-bit write with the low byte duplicated into the high byte, whether the address you wrote to was even or odd. So a byte write is not a byte write; it is a word write of a value you did not intend. Write words or longwords. A longword write is exactly two word writes on either port, and for the control port Sega says explicitly that the high half goes first, which is what makes the two-word address command work as a single move.l.

Gotcha A byte write to the data port writes a word. move.b #$40,(a6) aimed at $C00000 does not put $40 anywhere by itself: the VDP takes $4040. The manual's own text implies byte access works, and the correction is measurement, not documentation, which is exactly the kind of gap that makes this track insist on a second source for anything load-bearing.

One more rule, and it is the one most likely to bite somebody writing a VDP helper for the first time: according to plutiedev, setting a register clears whatever address you had pending, so the safe habit is to issue the address command again after any register write, before touching the data port. No other source we hold states it, and accurate emulator cores clear the pending operation code rather than the address, so read it as advice for writing a helper you can trust rather than as a measured rule about the silicon. It ships here attributed rather than flat, because a single source is honest when it is named and an impostor when it is not.

3 · The hazard: instructions that read before they write

This is the section the whole page owes its readers. The 68000 has a large family of instructions that quietly read their destination before writing it: they are called read-modify-write instructions, and on ordinary memory the extra read is invisible and harmless. Aimed at the VDP it is neither.

Sega states the mechanism in two bullets, and they fail in opposite directions:

  • A read while the port is set up for writing hangs the machine. In Sega's words, the VDP suspends the 68000, and it does that by never returning DTACK. The 68000 waits for that signal to know a bus cycle finished. It does not arrive, so the CPU waits forever. Nothing short of a hard reset recovers it.
  • A write while the port is set up for reading does nothing at all, except advance the address. No data is stored. There is no error, no flag, no symptom other than the picture being wrong later.

Read the first bullet again, because it is stronger than it looks: a wrong-mode read on its own is enough to hang the console. Nothing has to be written afterwards. That is the mechanism, and it is the reason the instruction list below is what it is.

Sega says VRAM, seven times on that page, and never once says CRAM, VSRAM, "data port" or "control port", so VRAM is the only scope that goes in Sega's mouth. In the wiki's own voice, the hazard is a property of the PORT'S DIRECTION rather than of VRAM specifically, and applies just as much when the pending target is CRAM or VSRAM. That is corroborated rather than assumed: the Genesis Plus GX emulator core, whose behaviour is built from hardware testing, rejects a data-port read while any of the three write codes is pending and rejects a data-port write while any of the three read codes is pending, with exactly Sega's two failures, and its comments say the first case normally locks the VDP until a hard reset.

Gotcha clr.w $C00000 hangs the machine. Not corrupts, not ignores: hangs, with the 68000 stalled waiting for a DTACK that never comes. CLR on this processor reads its destination before it writes the zero, and that read is the thing the VDP will not tolerate. It is also Sega's own worked example.

Sega then lists the instructions this rules out, grouped its own way. Nothing else anywhere enumerates this list, so it is worth saying plainly that the roster below is Sega's alone. Other sources warn about individual instructions, plutiedev's clr advice at the end of this section among them; nobody else enumerates the family:

Sega's groupInstructions

Twenty-six mnemonics. BTST is not among them, and that omission is correct rather than an oversight: BTST only reads, so it has nothing to write back. It is still subject to the mechanism above, which cares about direction rather than about writing.

CMPI is the oddity on that list. It reads twice and writes nothing, so Sega's own closing parenthetical, which justifies the whole roster on the grounds that "read and write operations are made to a single destination in these commands", is simply not true of it. The economical explanation is that Sega swept in the entire immediate group at once: the list is organised by addressing-mode family, not by bus behaviour. Treat CMPI as a curiosity of how the list was assembled, not as evidence for anything. The fact that a wrong-mode read alone is fatal comes from the mechanism paragraph, not from CMPI's presence.

The same page opens with a separate statement about TAS: it is not supported on the Genesis, and if you issue one the test happens but the write is ignored and never performed. Sega's wording is blanket, but the lost write is a bus-side failure, so what it costs you is the memory form; TAS aimed at a data register never leaves the CPU and does exactly what the manual describes. That is a different failure from the two above, and not a port hazard at all: it happens against any memory on the machine, the bus cycle completes normally, and the write is simply never performed. TAS is on Sega's roster for that separate reason as well as being subject to the direction rule like everything else on it, which makes it the extreme member of the family rather than a lone special case. The 68000 System page covers TAS as an instruction.

An independent second source reaches the same warning by its own route. plutiedev, teaching VDP setup, says not to use clr for it, because a quirk of the 68000 makes the instruction do a dummy read before writing back a zero and the VDP gets confused when that happens; and it generalises further than Sega does, to "don't use clr when dealing with any hardware registers". Same instruction, same mechanism, different publisher and different reasoning. The hazard is not a single-source claim.

New here? · Why would an instruction read something it is about to overwrite?

Because on the 68000 it is usually free, and because most of these instructions genuinely need the old value. addq.w #1,(a0) has to know what is there before it can add one. bset #3,(a0) has to keep the seven bits it is not touching. lsr.w (a0) has to have the bits before it can slide them.

clr is the one that surprises people, because logically it needs nothing: it is writing zero. The original 68000 reads anyway. It is a quirk of that chip's implementation, and later members of the family stopped doing it, which is why the habit survives in code written for other machines.

On real memory none of this matters. Reading a byte of work RAM and then writing it back costs a few cycles and changes nothing else. A port is different: reading it is an ACTION, with a direction the VDP has been told to expect, and doing it at the wrong moment is not a wasted cycle but a wrong move in a protocol.

Now the practical shape of it, which is why this page exists. Every instruction on that list is an ordinary, sensible 68000 idiom. CLR is how you zero something. Scc looks like a pure write and is not. The shifts and rotates operate on memory perfectly happily. ADDQ is the cheapest way to bump a value by one. Aim any of them at the VDP and you get a hang or a silent no-op. The fix is always the same and never clever: read the value into a data register, work on it there, and write it back with MOVE. If what you actually wanted was to fill video memory with zeros, that is a DMA fill's job, not CLR's.

4 · A plane is a grid of words

A plane is not a picture. It is a table. The VDP divides a plane into 8x8-pixel cells and keeps one 16-bit word per cell in VRAM, and that table is called a nametable. The word does not contain any pixels. It names a tile that lives elsewhere in VRAM and says four things about how to draw it there.

PRI
D15 PALETTE
D14-D13 V FLIP
D12 H FLIP
D11 TILE INDEX
D10-D0

High to low: one priority bit, a two-bit palette number (0 to 3, which of the four sixteen-colour palettes this cell paints with), a vertical flip bit, a horizontal flip bit, and an eleven-bit tile index. Eleven bits reach 0 to 2047, and 2047 is not an arbitrary ceiling: VRAM is 65,536 bytes and a tile is 32 of them, so 2048 tiles is exactly what fits. The field and the memory are sized to each other.

Gotcha Vertical flip is bit 12 and horizontal flip is bit 11, which is the reverse of the order most people guess, and an easy one to get backwards. If your tiles come out mirrored along the wrong axis, this is why.

The tile itself, in one line, because the tile format belongs to its own material: 8x8 pixels, 4 bits per pixel, 32 bytes, and the index in the word is a tile NUMBER rather than an address. The byte address is the index times 32, which works only because every tile is the same size. Sonic 2 spells that conversion out as its own helper, tiles_to_bytes, whose whole body is a mask down to eleven bits and a shift left by 5.

New here? · Why name a tile instead of just storing the pixels?

Count the bytes. A 320x224 screen is 71,680 pixels. At 4 bits each that is 35,840 bytes of pixels for one screenful, and the VDP only has 65,536 bytes in total, for everything, forever. A game that stored its picture as pixels would spend more than half its video memory on one still frame and have nothing left for a second one.

Now count it the other way. A screen is 40 by 28 cells, which is 1120 cells, and each cell costs 2 bytes: 2240 bytes for the whole screen map. The tiles it points at are shared, so a brick wall of 300 cells can be four distinct tiles used seventy-five times each. The map is small and the art is reused, and that is the entire economy of the machine.

The flip bits are part of the same bargain. A tile drawn mirrored is a second picture for no extra bytes, which is why so much Genesis art is symmetrical: the artist got the right half free.

Scrolling falls out of it too. Because the map is a grid of small numbers rather than a bitmap, moving the background is not a matter of moving any pixels at all. It is a number in a scroll table, which sections 6 and 7 are about.

The cells are stored row by row, left to right and then down, with the plane's top-left cell first. So a cell's offset into the table is (width * row + column) * 2, where width is the plane's width in cells, not the screen's. Sega's own worked table for a 32-wide plane runs $0000, $0002 and on to $003E across the first row, with $0040 starting the second.

The inner loop is one move.w per cell straight into the data port. The outer loop re-issues the address command once per row and adds a row's worth of address to it, because the rectangle being written is narrower than the plane, so the auto-increment cannot carry the address from the end of one row to the start of the next. $80 is one row of a 64-cell-wide plane: 64 cells times 2 bytes.

One bridge worth having before the sprite material: a sprite names its tiles with a word in exactly this format, priority, palette and flip bits included. The difference is scope. On a plane the word describes ONE cell; on a sprite the same bits apply to the whole sprite, however many tiles it is built from.

One tile on the screen, start to finish

Everything needed for a visible result is now on the table: the two ports, both forms of the command word, the auto-increment, and the nametable word. So here is the round trip in one piece, with nothing skipped and nothing deferred. It starts from a console that has just been switched on and ends with a square you can photograph. Every command word is derived in the prose beside it, from the layouts in section 2, so you can check the arithmetic rather than trust it.

Teaching example · not from a shipped game

Before the first line

Two assumptions, both of them what a real boot sequence does anyway. The console has already satisfied whatever security system it has, so the video chip is unlocked. And VRAM has been cleared to zero, which matters at the end: every cell this walkthrough does not write still holds the word $0000, which names tile 0, and tile 0 is 32 bytes of zero, which is 64 transparent pixels. Two address registers hold the ports, because every line below uses one of them:

    lea     ($C00000).l,a6      ; a6 = the data port
    lea     ($C00004).l,a5      ; a5 = the control port

Step 1 · eleven registers

Each of these is the register form of the command word: $8000 plus the register number shifted up eight places, plus the byte to store. Register 2 holding $30 is therefore the word $8230.

    move.w  #$8004,(a5)     ; reg 0:  no H interrupt, left column shown
    move.w  #$8144,(a5)     ; reg 1:  display ON, mode 5, 28-cell height
    move.w  #$8230,(a5)     ; reg 2:  plane A map at $C000
    move.w  #$8407,(a5)     ; reg 4:  plane B map at $E000
    move.w  #$857C,(a5)     ; reg 5:  sprite list at $F800
    move.w  #$8700,(a5)     ; reg 7:  backdrop = palette 0, colour 0
    move.w  #$8B00,(a5)     ; reg 11: both axes scroll as one whole screen
    move.w  #$8C81,(a5)     ; reg 12: H40, 320 pixels across
    move.w  #$8D2E,(a5)     ; reg 13: horizontal scroll table at $B800
    move.w  #$8F02,(a5)     ; reg 15: auto-increment 2
    move.w  #$9001,(a5)     ; reg 16: both planes 64 cells wide, 32 tall

Three of those values are arithmetic rather than flags. Register 2 wants plane A's address divided by $2000, sitting in bits 5 to 3: $C000 / $2000 is 6, and 6 shifted up three places is $30. Register 5 wants the sprite list divided by $200, and $F800 / $200 is $7C. Register 13 wants the scroll table divided by $400, and $B800 / $400 is $2E. The rest are bits. Register 12's $81 is the two H40 bits, which live at opposite ends of the byte and have to match each other. Register 1's $44 is the display-enable bit plus the fixed bit that says mode 5 rather than the Master System's mode 4. And register 15's 2 is what lets the runs of writes below carry their own addresses forward.

Step 2 · zero the scroll

Horizontal scroll lives in VRAM, in the table register 13 just placed, and it is already zero because VRAM was cleared. Vertical scroll lives in VSRAM, which nothing cleared, so it has to be written. The command is the address form carrying the VSRAM write code, %000101: CD1 and CD0 are 01, so the first word is those two bits over fourteen zero address bits, $4000; CD5 to CD2 are 0001, which sits in bits 7 to 4 of the second word, making it $0010. Sent high half first, that is one longword.

    move.l  #$40000010,(a5) ; VSRAM write, address $0000
    move.l  #0,(a6)         ; plane A and plane B: vertical scroll 0

Step 3 · three colours

A tile's pixels are palette indices, so nothing means anything until there are colours to index. The command is the address form again, this time with the CRAM write code %000011: CD1 and CD0 are both 1, so the first word begins 11 and carries an address of zero, and every CD bit above them is zero, so the second word is zero too. That is $C0000000.

    move.l  #$C0000000,(a5) ; CRAM write, address $0000
    move.w  #$0000,(a6)     ; colour 0: black, and this is the backdrop
    move.w  #$000E,(a6)     ; colour 1: red
    move.w  #$0EEE,(a6)     ; colour 2: white

Auto-increment 2 steps exactly one colour slot per word write, so those three land in slots 0, 1 and 2 of palette 0 without another command. A colour word packs three 3-bit channels, blue then green then red, and the colour material owns that format in full; all this needs is that $000E is as red as the chip goes and $0EEE is white.

Step 4 · the tile's 32 bytes

Tile 1 lives at byte $0020, because a tile is 32 bytes and 1 times 32 is $20. Tile 0 is deliberately left as it was. The command is the VRAM write code, %000001: CD1 and CD0 are 01, so the first word is 01 followed by the low 14 bits of $0020, which is $4020; the top two address bits are zero, so the second word is $0000; the longword is $40200000. Then the pixels, four bits each, two to a byte, left-hand pixel in the high nibble. This square is invented for the occasion, and the hex digits below ARE its pixels:

    move.l  #$40200000,(a5) ; VRAM write, address $0020
    move.l  #$22222222,(a6) ; 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 2
    move.l  #$21111112,(a6) ; 2 1 1 1 1 1 1 2
    move.l  #$21111112,(a6) ; 2 1 1 1 1 1 1 2
    move.l  #$21111112,(a6) ; 2 1 1 1 1 1 1 2
    move.l  #$21111112,(a6) ; 2 1 1 1 1 1 1 2
    move.l  #$21111112,(a6) ; 2 1 1 1 1 1 1 2
    move.l  #$21111112,(a6) ; 2 1 1 1 1 1 1 2
    move.l  #$22222222,(a6) ; 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 2

Eight longwords is 32 bytes, one row of eight pixels per line, and a longword write to the data port is exactly two word writes, so the auto-increment carries the address forward four bytes at a time all the way through. Colour 2 is the white border, colour 1 is the red fill.

Step 5 · one nametable word

Now put that tile somewhere. Plane A's map begins at $C000 and the plane is 64 cells wide, and cells are stored row by row, so the cell at row 13, column 20 sits at offset (64 x 13 + 20) x 2, which is $6A8, at address $C6A8. The command is the VRAM write code again: the low 14 bits of $C6A8 are $06A8, so the first word is $46A8, and the top two address bits are both 1, landing in the bottom two bits of the second word to make it $0003. The longword is $46A80003. The word being stored is $0001: priority bit clear, palette 0, neither flip set, tile index 1.

    move.l  #$46A80003,(a5) ; VRAM write, address $C6A8
    move.w  #$0001,(a6)     ; low priority, palette 0, no flips, tile 1

What you see. A black screen, 320 pixels across, with one 8x8 square near the middle of it: a red block inside a white outline, sitting perfectly still. Everything about that picture is now accounted for. It is black because every other cell of both planes still names tile 0, and tile 0 is transparent, so the backdrop colour shows through. The square is where it is because of one number, $C6A8. It is red and white because of two numbers in CRAM. And it stays on screen with the 68000 doing nothing at all from here on, because the VDP redraws it out of its own memory, every frame, without being asked again. Change the address in the command and the square moves. Leave the command alone and keep writing $0001, and the auto-increment lays the same tile along the rest of that row.

Each step above has a section of its own. The two ports, both command-word forms and the auto-increment are section 2. The five fields of that $0001 are the section you just read, and the composer above it builds the same word by clicking. Where the plane maps may live, and why register 2 took $30 rather than anything that looks like $C000, is section 5. The two scroll tables this example only zeroed are sections 6 and 7. The priority bit left clear in the nametable word is section 8. And the reason not one line above reaches for clr to zero anything is section 3.

Planes wrap, endlessly, in both directions. Scroll past the right edge of a 64-cell plane and cell 0 comes round again. New here? · What does it mean for a plane to wrap?

Imagine the plane's map printed on a cylinder, then that cylinder bent round into a doughnut so the top meets the bottom too. There is no edge anywhere on it. When the VDP works out which cell to fetch for a screen position, it takes the scroll value, adds the screen position, and then keeps only enough of the result to name a cell in the plane. Run off the right side and you arrive at the left side, in the same row, seamlessly.

That is not a feature somebody implemented; it is what happens when you keep the low bits of a number and throw the rest away. It is also why a level cannot just be one enormous plane: the widest a plane goes is 128 cells, which is 1024 pixels, and buying that width costs you every row past 32. Games instead keep the plane small and constantly rewrite the column of cells that is about to scroll into view, so the map is a moving window onto a much larger level held in ordinary work RAM or ROM.

Register writes are not a one-time boot ritual either. They can be changed part way down a frame, and a shipped game does exactly that. 6 · Scrolling sideways: the HScroll table

Horizontal scrolling is not done by moving anything. It is a number, added to the position the VDP is about to fetch from, and the interesting part is how MANY of those numbers there are. Register 11 has two bits that decide, and like the plane size they apply to both planes at once:

HSCRLSCRSega's nameHow many values
00full screenone pair, for the whole picture
01prohibitedsee below
10each 1 cellone pair per 8-pixel row of cells
11each 1 lineone pair per scanline

The values live in VRAM, in a table whose base is register 13, and they are interleaved: plane A first, then plane B, then plane A again, for the whole length of the table. A scanline is one horizontal line of the picture, and an NTSC display draws 224 visible ones NTSC / PAL, so the three modes work out as:

full screen24
per cell row56, being 28 rows times 2 planes112
per scanline448, being 224 lines times 2 planes896

Register 13 only carries the top six address bits, so the table always starts on a 1KB boundary, and no mode ever reaches past the end of that 1KB page: the longest read is per-line scrolling on a 240-line display V30 mode, at 960 bytes. That is where the habit of drawing the table as a flat 1KB block comes from, and Sega's own worked layout does exactly that.

The chip reserves nothing, though. It reads what its mode tells it to read and never touches the rest, so what the table really costs you is however many 32-byte tile slots it lands in. In full-screen mode that is one: four bytes at the base, and the other 31 tiles of the page are yours for art. In per-line mode on a 224-line display it is 28 tile slots, filled solid.

Cell mode is the interesting one, and it is the reason "1KB" survives as a rule of thumb. It reads one entry pair in eight, 112 bytes instead of 896, but it reads them 32 bytes apart, so it puts four bytes into the front of every one of those same 28 tile slots and leaves 28-byte holes between them. A tile is 32 bytes and has to start on a 32-byte boundary, so not one of those holes can hold anything. Cell mode costs exactly the same video memory as line mode. What it saves is the per-frame cost of getting the values there, and on this machine that is usually the scarcer resource.

New here? · How does one number per line make a background bend?

The VDP draws the picture one scanline at a time, top to bottom. Before it draws a line it needs to know where in the plane that line starts, and in per-line mode it looks that up fresh, from this table, for every single line.

So give line 0 the value 100, line 1 the value 101, line 2 the value 102, and the background slides one pixel further left with every line down the screen. The plane itself has not moved and no pixel has been rewritten. You have simply told the chip a different lie about where the row starts, 224 times.

Stack a few bands of different values and you get parallax: the distant clouds barely move, the middle hills move a bit, the ground under Sonic's feet moves fast. It costs one buffer in work RAM and one transfer per frame, and it is one of the most recognisable Genesis effects there is.

Each value is 10 bits, 0 to 1023; the top six bits of the word are ignored by the VDP, and Sega explicitly says software may use them for its own purposes. The range is not arbitrary: plane widths in pixels are 256, 512 or 1024, and all three divide 1024, so the scroll value wraps in step with the plane no matter which size you chose. Adding to the value shifts the plane to the RIGHT; subtracting shifts it left and wraps, so 0 minus 5 is 1019.

7 · Scrolling vertically: VSRAM

Vertical scrolling works the same way and lives somewhere else entirely. Its values are not in VRAM at all; they are in VSRAM, the 80-byte memory from section 1, reached through the same two ports with the VSRAM operation code instead of the VRAM one. Why it is a memory of its own rather than another table in VRAM is not something any source here settles, and the obvious guess does not survive contact with the previous section: the horizontal values are read at least as often and they stayed in VRAM.

One bit chooses the mode, VSCR in register 11. Clear it and the VDP reads the first pair of entries only, one for each plane, and the whole picture scrolls together. Set it and the VDP reads a value per column instead. On a 40-cell display that is all 40 entries, which is 20 column positions times 2 planes, and 20 is the key number: each vertical scroll value covers TWO cells, so the granularity is 16 pixels across, never 8. Adding to the value shifts the plane UP relative to the display, which is the opposite sense to the horizontal table, where adding shifts right.

New here? · Why would you want columns to scroll independently?

Because it buys effects that would otherwise cost a redraw. A row of pillars that sink into the floor at different rates, a wall that ripples, a lift shaft where one column of the background rises while the rest holds still, a title screen where letters drop in one strip at a time. Each is one number per 16-pixel column, written once per frame.

The classic use is not an effect at all, though. It is depth: give the background columns slightly different vertical offsets as the camera pans and the far layer appears to have parallax in both axes rather than only sideways.

Note what you cannot do with it. The chip gives you no per-line vertical scroll table the way it gives you a per-line horizontal one: VSRAM's granularity stops at the 16-pixel column. The only way past that is to rewrite VSRAM part way down the frame from a horizontal interrupt, a few lines' worth at a time, which is what the split screen in section 5 does. That asymmetry is why sideways effects are everywhere on the Genesis and vertical ones are rare.

Each entry is 11 bits wide as stored, and only 10 of those do anything unless the display is in the double-resolution interlace mode. The manual describes the top bits of the word as indefinite, so do not read anything into them.

The column -1 defect

Two-cell vertical scrolling has a documented failure at the very left edge of the screen, and it is worth stating precisely because it wears a confusing name. When two-cell vertical scrolling is combined with a horizontal scroll that is not on a cell boundary, the leftmost 16-pixel column can take the wrong vertical scroll value, and draws garbage.

The mechanism, from Kabuto's hardware testing, is that the VDP naturally renders two extra tiles to the left of the visible border, so that there are pixels available when the picture is scrolled to the right. That off-screen strip is nicknamed column -1. When the vertical scroll values are handed out per column and the horizontal scroll has pushed the columns out of alignment, column -1 can be fed the wrong one, and part of it is on screen.

Two independent sources describe the defect and its mechanism, so its existence is not in doubt. What varies is the folklore around it: one set of Mega Drive graphics notes, rasterscroll, records that developers typically hid the column behind a black bar made of sprites, and adds that a black bar creeping in from the left is often a sign that column scrolling is about to start. No game is named, and that is a claim about what programmers did rather than about what the chip does, so take it as colour. The hardware also offers its own answer, a register bit that blanks the leftmost 8 pixels outright, and that bit is section 10's business.

This is not the Window's boundary defect, which is a different mechanism in a different part of the chip. Section 10 exists to keep the two apart.

8 · Priority: one bit, seven layers

Start with what the priority bit is not, because the common wrong models all start from something it is not. It is not a depth value: there is no number to compare, only a bit that is set or clear. It is not a property of a plane: it lives in the nametable word, so one cell of plane A can be high while the cell next to it is low. And it is not a sprite ordering mechanism.

What it is: one bit per CELL and one bit per SPRITE. That is it. Each of the three things the VDP can draw, plane B, plane A and the sprites, is split in two by its own bit, and with the backdrop underneath everything that gives a stack of seven levels:

Front to backLevel
1sprites with priority set
2plane A cells with priority set
3plane B cells with priority set
4sprites with priority clear
5plane A cells with priority clear
6plane B cells with priority clear
7the backdrop colour

Read down it and the pattern is regular: sprites, A, B, then sprites, A, B again, then the backdrop. Setting a bit does not move a thing to the front; it moves it up one tier, from the lower group of three to the upper group of three, keeping its place within the group.

New here? · If it is one bit, how do you get seven layers?

Because the bit is not a layer number. There are only three things that can put a pixel on the screen: plane B, plane A, and the sprites. Their order among themselves is fixed and never changes: sprites in front, then plane A, then plane B. That is three layers, and no bit can reorder them.

What the bit does is split each of those three in half. Every plane cell and every sprite belongs either to the lower copy of its layer or to the upper copy, and all three upper copies sit in front of all three lower copies. Three layers, doubled, is six, plus the backdrop underneath everything makes seven.

The consequence people find surprising is what it does NOT buy you. Lifting a plane A cell into the upper group puts it in front of every sprite in the lower group, and behind every sprite in the upper group. You cannot put a plane cell in front of a high-priority sprite at all, because there is no eighth level for it to go to. If a game needs that, it draws the thing as a sprite.

And notice what the split is per. Not per plane: per CELL. Two neighbouring cells of plane A can be in different groups, which is how a foreground pillar and the wall behind it live in the same nametable.

Reference · Sega's eight-row priority truth table

Sega documents priority as a table rather than as a stack: for each combination of the three bits, the resulting front-to-back order of that one sprite, that one plane A cell, that one plane B cell and the background. All eight rows are the seven-level stack above with the levels nobody occupies removed, which is what makes the stack a safe simplification rather than a lossy one.

SpritePlane APlane BOrder, front to back

Worked check on the two rows that look surprising. With only plane B set (row 5) the stack ranks B at level 3, the sprite at level 4 and plane A at level 5, so B beats the sprite and the sprite beats A: B, S, A. With both planes set and the sprite clear (row 7) the ranks are A at 2, B at 3 and the sprite at 4: A, B, S. The background is always last, which is why it never moves in this table.

Three things people get wrong

"A high-priority plane tile draws above the sprites." Above the LOW-priority sprites, and below the high-priority ones. This is the misconception the stack exists to kill, and the vague version, "above most sprites", is worse than the wrong version because it sounds careful. The precise statement is the one the table gives: setting a plane cell's bit lifts it above every sprite that has not set its own.

"The sprite's priority bit decides which sprite is on top." It has nothing to do with sprite-versus-sprite ordering. Two sprites that overlap are ordered by their position in the sprite list, and a low-priority sprite can happily cover a high-priority one, which is a technique real games use for masking. The bit only describes the sprite's relationship to the background planes. Which sprite wins against which other sprite belongs to the sprite material.

"The Window is a fourth layer." It is not, and one widely-read reference has this wrong, listing the Window above sprites in its own tier. Four sources including Sega's own manual say the Window occupies plane A's place in this stack exactly. Section 9 is that fact in full.

There is no clean excerpt in any shipped Sonic disassembly that DEMONSTRATES a plane cell beating a sprite, which is worth saying rather than papering over. The priority bit is set constantly and commented almost never: it is the last argument of every make_art_tile call, and Sonic 2's main source file carries over a thousand lines that pass it as a bare 0 or 1 with no explanation attached. So here is an invented one, showing what a game is saying when it sets it:

Teaching example · not from a shipped game 
; a foreground pillar the player walks BEHIND: plane A, priority set
	dc.w	make_block_tile($120,0,0,2,1)	; $C120

; ordinary background wall on the same plane: priority clear
	dc.w	make_block_tile($121,0,0,2,0)	; $4121

; the player object, priority clear, so the pillar covers it
	move.w	#make_art_tile($780,0,0),art_tile(a0)

Two cells of the same plane, two rows apart in the same table, and they behave differently: the pillar draws over the player and the wall draws under it. Nothing about the plane changed. One bit in one word did.

One priority behaviour belongs elsewhere but is worth flagging here so it is not a surprise: when the shadow and highlight mode is enabled, a high-priority background cell also forces everything in its 8x8 area to normal brightness, whether or not the cell contains any opaque pixels. That interaction is the colour material's business.

9 · The Window: the plane that replaces plane A

The Window plane is a third tilemap with one job: hold still. It never scrolls. It is pinned to the display, so a score line or a status bar drawn on it stays put no matter what the rest of the picture is doing. It has its own nametable, separate from plane A's, with its own base register.

Where the Window is displayed, plane A is not. This is the load-bearing fact of the whole section and the one most often got wrong. The Window is not composited over plane A: it takes plane A's place, in that region, entirely. Sega's manual says plane A is not displayed in the area where the Window is displayed. Charles MacDonald's notes say the two cannot overlap and that the Window is treated exactly the same as plane A for priority. plutiedev says they share a layer. rasterscroll says the VDP treats them as the same plane for priority purposes. Four sources, no disagreement, and it has a consequence you can see: a transparent pixel in a Window cell does not reveal plane A underneath. It reveals plane B, or the backdrop.

In the priority stack of section 8, the Window sits exactly where plane A sits. It is not a fourth layer, it does not float above sprites, and a Window cell's own priority bit works exactly like a plane A cell's.

New here? · If it replaces plane A, what is it actually for?

Think about what a scrolling game needs at the top of the screen: rings, time, score, lives. Those are drawn from tiles like everything else, and if they were on plane A they would slide away the moment the camera moved.

There are three ways out. Draw the display as sprites, which costs sprite slots and sprite pixels on the busiest scanlines. Rewrite the plane A cells under the display every frame to cancel the scroll, which costs work and goes wrong at the edges. Or turn on the Window over those rows, which costs one register write and nothing per frame.

The price is that plane A genuinely stops existing there, so whatever the Window shows has to be everything you want in that rectangle. That is why the Window is usually a solid band at the top or bottom of the screen rather than a floating panel: a floating panel would punch a hole in the background.

The Window's shape is fixed, not chosen. Its nametable is 32 by 32 cells in the 256-pixel display mode and 64 by 32 in the 320-pixel one, which is 2KB and 4KB respectively, and it follows the display width rather than any register. The base register is number 3, in units of $800, with one extra rule: in the wider mode its lowest address bit, WD11, must be zero, so the table has to be $1000-aligned there.

Two registers place it, and each holds a direction bit plus a split position:

RegisterDirection bitSplit position
17 ($91)RIGT: clear puts the Window LEFT of the split, set puts it rightfive bits, in units of 2 cells, so 16 pixels
18 ($92)DOWN: clear puts the Window ABOVE the split, set puts it belowfive bits, in units of 1 cell, so 8 pixels

The two combine, so a Window can be a band, a corner block or an L shape rather than only a strip. Writing zero to both registers turns it off: the split is at position 0 with the "left" and "above" sense, so the Window occupies nothing. That is the standard way to switch it off, which is why $9100 and $9200 appear in so many boot tables. Sonic 2 writes exactly those two values and never uses the Window for anything.

Gotcha The manual contradicts itself about this register pair, twice. One footnote on the register page gives the horizontal position in units of one cell; the chapter text, the worked examples and every outside source say two. And one sentence in the Window chapter says the Window's nametable base is register 13; three other places in the same manual say register 3, and register 13 is the horizontal scroll table. The correct values are the ones above. This is a good thing to have met once: a first-party document is the best source available and is still a document, written by people, and the only defence is reading a second one.

The Window boundary defect, exactly

There is a real hardware defect at the Window's vertical edge, and it has a narrow trigger. When the Window is anchored to the LEFT and plane A is scrolled horizontally by an amount that is not a multiple of 16 pixels, the two cells of plane A immediately to the RIGHT of the Window boundary fetch the wrong tiles and draw garbage.

Sega documents the symptom and its extent, in its own words: only when the Window is set to the left and plane A is moved in the horizontal direction, the character corresponding to two cells on the right side of the boundary will be disfigured. It also documents both escapes. There is no malfunction when the Window is on the left and plane A moves only vertically, and none when the Window is anchored to the right. Sega's own workaround, printed as a caption on its diagram, is to mask those two cells with high-priority plane A tiles.

Sega does not give the 16-pixel part of the trigger. One set of community VDP notes does, and it is the only source we hold that does, so that half of the statement above rests on a single leg and is flagged rather than folded quietly into Sega's account. It is worth flagging twice over, because the same notes are the reference section 8 catches putting the Window above sprites. The two accounts do not contradict each other: Sega gives the symptom and the extent, those notes give the alignment condition, and the merged rule above is stated in this page's own voice.

Neither of the sources we hold shows the defect. Both describe it and neither prints a picture, so what is above is the symptom exactly as sourced, and no further: which two cells, which side, and what turns it off.

Shipped games used the Window sparingly. Sonic 2 never uses it at all: the only writes to either position register anywhere in its source are $9100 and $9200, both of which switch it off. Sonic 3 and Knuckles uses it rarely, and the two uses are the two obvious shapes.

10 · Three things called "the left column bug"

The left-hand edge of a Genesis screen has a bad reputation, and the phrase "the left column bug" gets attached to at least three unrelated things. Two are defects with different mechanisms in different parts of the chip, and the third is a deliberate feature at the screen's left edge. Conflating them is the easiest way to end up debugging the wrong one, and they do not even happen in the same place: one of the three has nothing to do with the left edge at all.

NameWhat it actually isTaught in
The Window boundary defectThe two cells of plane A immediately right of a LEFT-anchored Window draw the wrong tiles, when plane A is scrolled horizontally off a 16-pixel multiple. Nothing to do with the screen edge; it happens wherever the boundary is.Section 9
The VSRAM column -1 defectThe leftmost 16-pixel column of the screen takes the wrong vertical scroll value, when two-cell vertical scrolling is combined with a horizontal scroll that is off a cell boundary.Section 7
Left column blankNot a bug at all. Register 0, bit 5, deliberately blanks the leftmost 8 pixels of the display. It is the hardware's own answer to artefacts at the very left edge, which is where the column -1 defect shows.here

The third one deserves its explanation here since nowhere else claims it. Setting bit 5 of register 0 turns the leftmost 8-pixel column of the picture off: it draws as the backdrop colour, whatever the planes and sprites have there. Sega's register diagram prints that bit as a plain zero and documents nothing; plutiedev documents what it does, so it ships here attributed to plutiedev rather than as a flat fact.

Eight pixels is a cheap price for a column that cannot show an artefact at the screen's edge. Note what it does not reach, though, because it is why this section keeps the three apart: the Window boundary defect is not a left-edge phenomenon. It damages the two cells to the right of the Window boundary, and that boundary sits at the horizontal position register's split, which is at least 16 pixels in, because a split of zero means the Window occupies nothing. Blanking the first 8 pixels cannot touch it. Sega's remedy for that one is the high-priority mask in section 9, and no register bit substitutes for it.

The lesson of this section is the naming discipline rather than any one of the three facts. When somebody says "the left column bug", the first question is always which one, and the second is whether they mean a defect or the fix for one.
