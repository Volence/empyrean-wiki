The Genesis

The Machine

Five headline functions, three buses, one crystal, and a sixteen-megabyte map that says which chip answers at which address. This is the front door of the hardware track: what a Sega Genesis is made of, how the parts are wired to one another, what happens when a program reaches for an address nothing is listening at, and how the whole thing moves through one frame. Everything here is true of the silicon rather than of any one game's conventions, and it describes a bare console with nothing plugged into the expansion port.

Most of this page is true of every Genesis ever built. Where it is not, the claim carries a small badge naming what it depends on: NTSC / PAL for a figure that changes with the console's region, Board revision for behaviour that changes with which machine you opened, and Bare console for something that holds only with nothing plugged into the expansion port.

1 · One machine, five headline functions

Open a Sega Genesis and the interesting part is small. Two processors, one video chip, two sound generators, and a handful of glue parts to wire them together. Everything else in the case is power, a cartridge slot, two controller sockets and an expansion port. Five headline functions are worth learning by name, and they do not map one to one onto packages: only four of them are separate chips, because one of the sound generators lives inside the video chip, and how many packages the rest occupy depends on which revision of the board you opened Board revision. The glue gets named too, a little further down, because the rest of this track keeps leaning on it.

The wires come first, though, because the wires are what decide which part can talk to which and who has to wait for whom. A bus is the shared set of wires carrying addresses and data between chips. Several chips are attached to the same wires at once, so "who is on this bus" is never the whole question; the question is who is doing what on it at this instant, and there are four different answers.

  • Connected. The chip has an interface attached to those wires. Being connected says nothing at all about who is driving them.
  • Bus master. The device currently initiating a transaction: it is the one driving the address and control signals. One master at a time, and that is the grain of truth inside the usual sentence about only one device being able to use a bus at once.
  • Selected target. The addressed device that responds, including driving the data lines during a read. One target at a time as well.
  • Listener. Every other connected device sees the signals go past and stays electrically silent.

Mastership is handed over rather than grabbed, and the handing over has a name of its own: arbitration. Motorola's documentation for the 68000 keeps the two ideas apart, so this track does too. Being the master is a state a device is in; arbitration is the mechanism that decides who is in it next, and on this machine one of the glue parts named further down exists to do exactly that. The distinction is the reason the wiring is worth learning before the parts are: two chips connected to the same bus take turns at being master and can hold each other up, while two chips on different buses genuinely run at the same time.

A Genesis has three buses. Most parts are connected to exactly one of them. Of the five headline functions drawn in the diagram below, one is connected to two, and that turns out to be the most useful fact in this section:

  • A 16-bit system bus. The 68000 and the VDP are on it; so are the work RAM and the cartridge. This is the bus the memory map in section 3 describes.
  • An 8-bit local bus, the Z80's. The Z80, the YM2612 and the eight kilobytes of sound RAM are on it, along with the hardware that moves the Z80's window onto the other bus.
  • The video bus, which runs from the VDP to its video memory. Nothing but the VDP and VRAM is attached to it, and the colour and scroll memories are not even out here: they sit on the VDP's own die.

So of the five headline functions on this diagram, the VDP is the one connected to two buses. It is a single chip with two separate interfaces. Its system-bus interface is how the 68000 reaches its ports; its video-bus interface is how it reaches VRAM. Being connected to two buses is not owning two buses, and the distinction is worth holding onto, because what the second interface buys the VDP is a set of wires no processor is attached to. That is not a quirk of the diagram, it is the VDP's job: it is the bridge between the processor's world and the picture's. Everything awkward about talking to the video chip follows from it. The 68000 cannot reach across into video memory, because it has no interface on that bus and no wires that go there, which is why no address anywhere in its sixteen-megabyte map is a byte of video memory. Anything the 68000 wants drawn has to be handed to the VDP over the system bus and carried the rest of the way by the VDP itself, through the very small opening section 6 is about.

New here? · Who is allowed to talk on a bus?

Take the definition apart. One group of the wires carries an address, saying which location the initiator wants; another group carries the data going to or coming from that location; a few more carry timing and direction signals. Every chip connected to the bus sees all of it at once.

The catch is that any one wire may only be driven by one device at a time. If two chips both put a value on the data lines, the result is not two values, it is nonsense. So each transaction has exactly two active participants and a gallery: the master, which puts out the address and says whether it is reading or writing, and the target it addressed, which answers. Everyone else connected watches without driving anything.

That leaves the question of how a device becomes the master when two of them want to be, and the answer is not that they take it in turns politely. There is a part on the board whose only job is to decide, and it is the arbiter named later in this section.

That is why "which bus is this chip on" is a real question with real consequences, and not just a diagram detail. Two chips on the same bus take turns and can hold each other up. Two chips on different buses genuinely run at the same time, and neither has to wait unless one of them reaches into the other's bus. The Genesis gets both: the Z80 and its FM chip work away undisturbed on their own wires, and the moment either processor reaches across, somebody stalls.

Now the parts, each one on the wires it hangs off.

The 68000 is the main processor, and it runs the game: physics, collision, objects, menus, the lot. It sits on the system bus, which is where it finds everything it needs: the cartridge it fetches its instructions and its data straight out of, the work RAM it keeps its variables in, the video chip it sends the picture to, and the I/O chip the controllers plug into. It moves 16 bits at a time to and from the outside world and names a 24-bit byte-address space, which is what fixes that world at sixteen megabytes, and section 3 is the map of what answers where inside it.

The Z80 is the second processor, and it is a computer in its own right rather than a helper: its own bus, its own memory, its own program, its own clock. On a Genesis it conventionally runs the sound driver, and once the 68000 has loaded that driver into its RAM, the Z80 steps through it on its own wires with neither processor checking on the other. It is not sealed off, though. The Z80 names 64 kilobytes of address space, and the two halves of it do different jobs. The lower 32 kilobytes are the local Z80 address space, which is not 32 kilobytes of memory: there are eight kilobytes of Z80 RAM down there, mirrored within the range, and sharing it are the YM2612's registers, the hardware that moves the window, and ranges Sega reserved. The upper 32 kilobytes are that window, a movable view onto the 68000's bus, which is how the Z80 reaches cartridge ROM, and every time it looks through it somebody waits.

The VDP, the Video Display Processor, draws the picture, and it draws all of it: once a program has told it what to put on screen, it produces every pixel by itself, to the television's schedule, without the 68000's help. Its system-bus interface is on the same wires as everything else, so the 68000 can talk to it. What it draws from, though, is memory of its own that no other chip can address, out on the video bus its second interface is the only way onto. Section 6 is about those memories and the very small keyhole through which the 68000 reaches them.

The two sound chips are where the wiring gets interesting, because they are not on the same wires. The YM2612 is the FM synthesiser, the source of the sound people mean when they say a game sounds like a Mega Drive, and it hangs off the Z80's local bus, right next to the Z80 itself. One of its channels can be handed over to a DAC that plays recorded audio a byte at a time, which is how drums and voice clips get out of a cartridge, and which means the Z80 has to go and fetch those bytes from the cartridge on the far side of the machine. The PSG is far simpler, making plain tones and noise rather than instruments. And it is not a separate chip at all: the PSG core is built into the VDP's die, which puts the thing that makes the beeps inside the thing that draws the picture, on the 68000's bus rather than the Z80's.

Those are the five headline functions. The glue is two more parts, and neither ever appears in a screenshot. An I/O chip carries the version register, the three ports (two for controllers, one for expansion) and the serial lines. Both of those words are worth pinning down now, because the rest of the track uses them constantly. A register is a small storage slot inside a chip: the ones inside a processor hold the values it is working on, and the ones inside a hardware chip like the VDP say how that chip should behave. A port is either a socket on the case, like the two a controller plugs into, or an address a chip listens at, like the two the video chip is reached through; both senses are standard, and this page uses both.

The other glue part is a bus arbiter, and it sits between the two processors. When the 68000 wants the Z80's bus, or the Z80 reaches out into the 68000's, the arbiter is the part that actually stalls one and lets the other through. The two processors are not wired to each other's pins. They are wired to the arbiter, and it is the only way across.

New here? · Why would a console have two brains?

Because two of the jobs a game has to do run on completely different kinds of schedule, and one of the two does not bend.

Game logic is bursty. Some frames are cheap and some frames are a boss fight with forty objects on screen, and the work simply takes as long as it takes.

Sound is not bursty, and it is worth being exact about why, because the usual explanation gets it backwards. Neither sound chip is waiting to be fed. Tell the FM chip to play a note, or give the PSG a tone and a volume, and the chip goes on producing it until something tells it otherwise, whether the program is idle or buried in collision code, so silence is not what happens when a driver runs late. What a driver does on a schedule is change things: start and stop notes, step envelopes and vibrato along, service the chips' timers, and above all hand out the bytes of a recorded sample one at a time and evenly, because on this machine the rate the driver writes those bytes at is the sample's playback rate and nothing buffers the stream for it. That last job is the genuinely deadline-bound one. A note change that arrives late is a note that arrives late; a sample byte that arrives late stretches the waveform, and you hear it.

So the case for a second processor is scheduling and offload rather than rescue. Give the sound a processor of its own, with its own memory and its own wires, and the driver keeps its schedule and costs the game nothing, instead of competing for time with whatever this particular frame turned out to cost. Neither one has to know what the other is doing.

The particular reasons this machine put its sound on a Z80, rather than somewhere else or nowhere at all, are a story with real sources behind it and it is not settled on this page. What matters here is the shape: two processors, each with memory of its own, and an arbiter in between.

That layout puts the two sound chips on opposite sides of the machine, and the consequences run in both directions. The YM2612 hangs off the Z80's local bus, so the 68000 cannot touch it without taking the Z80's bus away first. The PSG is inside the VDP on the 68000's bus, so the Z80 cannot touch it without reaching out across the arbiter into the 68000's world. Sound is not cleanly "the Z80's department": it is split down the middle by the wiring, and half of it lives inside the video chip.

Nothing on this page depends on that fact yet. Everything later does. It is why a rule about the video chip can turn into a rule about audio, why a long transfer into video memory has consequences for the beeps, and why the two sound chips are programmed in completely different styles.

Carry forward
  • Five headline functions by name: the 68000 runs the game, the Z80 conventionally runs the sound driver, the VDP draws the picture, the YM2612 is the FM synthesiser, and the PSG makes the simple tones from inside the VDP's own die.
  • Three buses: the 16-bit system bus, the Z80's 8-bit local bus, and the video bus, which nothing but the VDP and VRAM is attached to. The VDP has an interface on two of the three, which is what makes it the bridge to the picture.
  • Four roles on any one bus, and they are not the same thing: every attached device is connected, one device is the master driving the transaction, one is the target answering it, and the rest listen without driving anything. Mastership changes hands by arbitration.
  • Two glue parts: an I/O chip carrying the version register and the three ports, and a bus arbiter between the two processors, so either one reaching into the other's world stalls somebody.

2 · One crystal, every clock

Everything in this section is a clock, so start there. A clock is a signal that flips between two voltages over and over, at a fixed rate. It carries no information at all. Its whole job is to be a metronome: on each tick, every chip listening to it moves one step further through whatever it was doing. One of those ticks is one cycle, which is the unit every timing figure on this track is counted in, and a megahertz is a million ticks a second, so 7.67 MHz is about 7,670,000 of them every second. The ticking itself comes from a crystal, a sliver of quartz that vibrates at a very stable frequency when you put a voltage across it. That crystal is the oscillator the next paragraph means.

There is one oscillator in a Genesis, and every clock in this section is that oscillator divided by a whole number. Not approximately, not nominally: exactly, by an integer.

NTSC / PAL On an NTSC console the master clock runs at 53,693,175 Hz. On a PAL console it runs at 53,203,424 Hz. From there: the 68000 gets one seventh of it, the Z80 gets one fifteenth, the YM2612 gets the same seventh the 68000 does, and the PSG gets the same fifteenth as the Z80.

Two figures in that picture come from Sega and the rest do not, and the difference is worth keeping straight. Of the clocks in this tree, Sega printed only the CPU's: 7.67 MHz on an NTSC console and 7.60 MHz on a PAL one. The two crystal values did not come from Sega either. The NTSC one is given identically, to eight digits, by three independently written emulator codebases; the PAL one is the figure three of four sources give, and the fourth is settled against by the arithmetic in the reference box below. Every divisor is the scene's, worked back out of the hardware. What makes all of it trustworthy is that it reproduces Sega's two printed figures exactly: 53,693,175 divided by 7 is 7,670,453.6 Hz, which is 7.67 MHz at the precision Sega quoted, and 53,203,424 divided by 7 is 7,600,489.1 Hz, which is 7.60 MHz. So the right way to say it is that the 68000 runs at one seventh of the master clock, and not that Sega divides the master clock by seven.

Because every clock is the same crystal divided, no two parts of the machine can drift apart. They are locked in a fixed ratio for as long as the console is powered: the 68000 gets exactly 15 of its cycles for every 7 the Z80 gets, on every console, at every temperature, forever. That is the two divisors turned upside down, and it is worth reading twice, because the 7 and the 15 swap chips between the two sentences. Dividing by the smaller number is what makes a chip faster, so the 68000, on master over 7, is the one that racks up 15 cycles in the time the Z80 takes to rack up 7. It is a genuinely useful property, because a timing relationship you measure once holds always.

NTSC / PAL It also means a PAL console is not a differently tuned machine. Its crystal is about 0.9 percent slower than an NTSC one, and every single clock in the machine comes down by that same 0.9 percent together. The large speed difference players notice between a PAL and an NTSC game is a separate effect entirely, and it comes from how a frame is built rather than from this fraction of a percent.

New here? · What is a cycle count actually counting?

A 68000 instruction costs some whole number of ticks, and different instructions cost different numbers of them. That is why code on this track is measured in cycles rather than in seconds: the count belongs to the instructions themselves and does not change from console to console.

Knowing the tick rate is what turns a count into a time. "This routine takes 300 cycles" becomes "this routine takes 39 microseconds" only once you know that 7.67 MHz is 7,670,000 ticks a second, near enough. That conversion is why cycle counts are worth counting at all, and it is also why it matters that the real figure is 7.67 and not the 8 MHz on the spec sheet further down.

One caution about the crystal. Stable is the operative word, and it is not the same as exact. The eight digits above are the frequency the part is cut for; any individual console sits a hair off it and moves a little as the case warms up. What does not move is the relationship between the chips, because that is division, and division does not drift.

One number in that tree is worth staring at. The Z80's clock, 53,693,175 divided by 15, comes out at 3,579,545 Hz with no remainder at all. That figure is the NTSC colourburst frequency, the reference every NTSC television signal is built around, and the multiplication runs the other way just as cleanly: 3,579,545 x 15 = 53,693,175. True colourburst is 315/88 MHz, which is 3,579,545.4545 and so on forever, so the figure in the tree is that number rounded down, and the Z80 ends up about half a hertz under true colourburst. What the arithmetic does not say is why. No Sega document found states a reason for the ratio, or says the crystal was chosen for it, so the relationship is stated here as a fact about the numbers and nothing further is claimed from it.

New here? · Why divide one crystal instead of giving each chip its own?

The obvious alternative is one oscillator per chip, each cut to the frequency that chip wants. It would work. What it would cost is the relationship between them.

Two independent crystals are never quite in the ratio you ordered. Manufacturing tolerance, temperature and age all move them a little, and they move them by different amounts, so the ratio between the two chips wanders slowly and unrepeatably. Anything you measured about how their timings line up would be true today and slightly untrue tomorrow.

Divide one crystal instead and the ratio is not approximately fixed, it is exactly fixed, because the division is counting. A divide-by-seven circuit emits one pulse for every seven it receives, and there is no way for that to be 6.98. Whatever else moves, the parts stay in step with each other, and code can rely on that. It is also, as it happens, one part instead of several.

A shipped game carries the same arithmetic in its own source. This is the clock section of Sonic 2's constants file, and it is the whole tree in five lines. Nothing here is an instruction; these are names the assembler works out before the ROM exists:

; ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
; Clocks
Master_Clock    = 53693175
M68000_Clock    = Master_Clock/7
Z80_Clock       = Master_Clock/15
FM_Sample_Rate  = M68000_Clock/(6*6*4)
PSG_Sample_Rate = Z80_Clock/16
s2disasm/s2.constants.asm:2116-2122

The first line is the crystal, written as a plain integer number of hertz. The next two are the two divisors, and they are the same 7 and 15 the tree above shows. The last two go one step further, past the clock a chip is fed and into what the chip does with it. 6*6*4 is 144, so the FM sample rate is the master clock divided by 1008, about 53,267 samples a second: the YM2612 divides its input by six and then spends 24 slots producing one sample. The PSG line divides the Z80's clock by a further 16 to reach the PSG's internal tone clock, about 223,722 Hz. Neither of those last two figures appears in any Sega document; like the divisors, they are back-computed, and this file is a shipped game showing its working.

Gotcha · the 8 MHz Genesis You will see "68000 at 8 MHz, Z80 at 4 MHz" everywhere, and whoever told you that can cite Sega for it. The Genesis Technical Overview prints exactly those figures on its specification-summary page. The same document, describing the version register, prints "NTSC CPU clock 7.67 MHz". Both are Sega's. The 8 and the 4 are the parts' nominal ratings, what those chips are sold as being able to run at, and not the clocks this console applies to them. The applied clocks are one seventh and one fifteenth of the crystal, which is 7.67 MHz and 3.58 MHz. The two errors are not the same size: 8 MHz is 4.3 percent above the 68000's real clock, while 4 MHz is about 11.7 percent above the Z80's. If you are checking a timing calculation against a spec sheet, this is an easy way to be wrong and not know it.

3 · The 68000's map of the world

Addresses start here, so a word about how they are written. Every address and value from this point on is in hexadecimal, base 16, marked by a leading $ that is notation and never part of the number. One hex digit stands for exactly four bits, so the six digits of $FF0000 are 24 bits of address. The numbers and notation page has a live converter if you would rather read any of them in another base.

The 68000 has a 16-bit data bus and a 24-bit byte-address space, and those two numbers meet in a detail worth having straight. The physical address signals are A23 through A1, twenty-three of them; the lowest bit of a byte address is not a pin at all but is carried by two byte strobes, which say whether the processor wants the high byte of a 16-bit word, the low byte, or both. Put together, that names 2^24 distinct byte addresses: $000000 through $FFFFFF, sixteen megabytes. Almost none of that is memory. On this machine an address is a question put to the whole system bus at once, and the memory map is the list of which chip is listening for it.

Sega drew that map in its own manual and it is worth learning in Sega's shape. Cartridge ROM at the bottom, from $000000 up to $400000. Then a box labelled SEGA RESERVED, and then a second, separate box also labelled SEGA RESERVED. Then SYSTEM I/O at $A00000, which holds the Z80's world, the controller ports and a handful of control registers. Then a third reserved box. Then the VDP at $C00000. Then, at the top, WORK RAM, drawn with everything from $E00000 to $FEFFFF marked ACCESS PROHIBITED and the RAM itself at $FF0000.

New here? · What is a memory map?

A processor has one set of address wires and no idea what is on the other end of them. When it wants a value it puts a number on those wires and waits for something to answer. It does not know whether the answer will come from a memory chip, a video chip or a controller port, and it does not care: an address is just a number it broadcasts.

So somebody has to decide, when the console is designed, which chip listens for which numbers. That decision is the memory map, and it is made in the wiring, not in software. Writing the number $C00004 on the address wires reaches the video chip on a Genesis because a designer wired the video chip to notice addresses that start with $C. Nothing about the number itself says "video".

This is why the same instruction can store a value in memory or command a hardware chip, with no difference in the code at all. The instruction is identical; the address decides who hears it. It is called memory-mapped I/O, and it is why the map below repays learning.

Three things can happen when the 68000 puts an address on the bus, and the difference between the second and the third is the difference between a bug you can chase and a console you have to unplug.

Some addresses work. A chip is listening, it answers, and the transfer completes. That is ROM, work RAM, the VDP's ports, the I/O registers. Working means the bus cycle completes, not that every access does what you meant: cartridge ROM answers every read and quietly discards every write, because it is read-only memory, and the 68000 is told nothing either way.

Some addresses answer with junk. Nothing out there is driving the data lines, so what comes back is whatever was already floating on them. What exactly floats there is not a constant of nature and is not promised to be the same on every machine: it is a side effect of the board's wiring rather than a specified behaviour, so it can differ with the board revision and with what is plugged in Board revision. The precise description on the machines Charles MacDonald tested, from his hardware-tested notes, is that a read returns "the MSB of the next instruction to be fetched, with the LSB set to zero": in plainer words the top byte of the instruction the 68000 was about to fetch anyway, with a zero byte underneath it. Writes go nowhere. Nothing traps, nothing is logged, and the program carries on holding a number it believes it read from somewhere. That is open bus, and the fact that it is not treated as an error is exactly what makes it dangerous, whichever pattern of junk a particular console hands back.

Teaching example · not engine code

A game has no reason to read an unmapped address, so there is no shipped excerpt to quote here. This is what it would look like if you did, on a console with no expansion hardware attached:

    move.w  ($500000).l,d0     ; d0 now holds junk, and nothing said so
    move.w  #$1234,($500000).l ; this write goes nowhere at all
    tst.w   d0                 ; the flags describe the junk, perfectly happily

The instructions are ordinary and correct. That third one tests the value it was handed and sets the flags, which are the few bits the 68000 keeps to describe the last result it saw, and which a later instruction can branch on. They describe the junk as faithfully as they would describe a real number, because the 68000 has no way to tell you the first line answered with garbage: from its side the bus cycle completed normally. This is how a stray pointer corrupts a game quietly, three frames after the actual mistake.

And some addresses hang the machine. The 68000 does not finish a bus cycle on its own schedule. It puts the address out and then waits for the addressed device to raise a signal called DTACK, meaning "done". If nothing out there is listening, nothing ever raises it, and the 68000 waits. Not for a while: forever. There is no timeout in the chip, no exception, no vector, no error screen and no way back. Meanwhile the VDP carries on drawing the picture that is already in its memory and the Z80 carries on playing music, which is why a hung Genesis so often looks alive.

New here? · Why can the CPU not just notice that nobody answered?

Because it has no way to tell the difference between "nobody is there" and "somebody is there and is being slow".

Here is the mechanism, at its dumbest. The 68000 wants a value. It puts the address on the address wires, says "read", and then it waits. It is waiting for one particular wire to be pulled low by whatever chip owns that address, which is the chip's way of saying "the data is on the wires now, take it". That wire is called DTACK, for data transfer acknowledge.

Waiting is the correct behaviour, and it is what makes the machine work at all. Memory chips of different speeds can share one bus precisely because each one takes as long as it needs and tells the processor when it is ready. A slow device is not an error; it is a device that raises DTACK late.

So when the 68000 addresses a hole, it does not see an error. It sees a device that has not finished yet, and it does the same thing it would do for a slow ROM: it waits. Nothing ever arrives, and nothing ever will, and it goes on waiting. This is also why "the console froze" and "the game crashed" are genuinely different failures on this machine, which section 5 comes back to.

Every trap in the reveals is stated in this section's prose too, so a reader who clicks nothing is never left holding one.

Which regions do which, and both of the hangs below rest on one chain of evidence apiece, so they are worth stating with their provenance attached. Charles MacDonald's hardware-tested notes record that $800000-$9FFFFF, the second of Sega's reserved boxes, locks the machine on any access, read or write, on a standard console Bare console; that is the range the 32X later mapped itself into, and although other sources list the region, MacDonald's is the one that reports the hazard. $B00000-$BFFFFF has nothing to assert DTACK on a standard console either, so accesses there hang as well Bare console, and that one is reported by two people in a single SpritesMind hardware thread rather than by two independent chains of evidence. The first reserved box behaves differently, and Sega drawing the two as separate boxes turns out to match the hardware: $400000-$7FFFFF answers with open-bus junk on a bare console Bare console and lets the program keep running. It is the expansion window, which is where a Sega CD maps itself when one is attached.

The traps inside regions that otherwise work are the ones that catch people, and they are single-sourced too. Charles MacDonald's hardware-tested notes record that two windows inside the Z80 area, $A07F00-$A07FFF and $A0FF00-$A0FFFF, lock the machine when the 68000 reads or writes them. plutiedev states that accessing $C00020 to $C0003F hangs up, which is also what MacDonald's rule for how the VDP decodes addresses predicts: the VDP answers only to addresses matching a particular bit pattern and locks up on the rest, which is likewise why its ports appear to repeat at many addresses that are not the canonical ones. And two ports inside the VDP's own block are traps in their own right, which is the next box.

One caution covering every hazard in this section. Not one of them is behaviour Sega documented. Sega's map marks these ranges reserved or prohibited and stops there; everything this page says about what actually happens when you touch them was found by somebody testing hardware. Behaviour of that kind is not guaranteed to be uniform, and it can differ with the board revision and with whatever is attached to the cartridge slot and the expansion port Board revision. Read each hazard as "this is what the machines somebody tested did", which is the strongest claim the sources support, rather than as a promise about the console in front of you. The safe conclusion does not depend on the details anyway: stay inside the addresses the map names.

Gotcha · the two VDP addresses that only work one way The PSG's port at $C00011 sits in the middle of the VDP's address block, next to ports you read all the time, and it cannot be read at all. Its neighbour is the mirror image: the H/V counter at $C00008 can only be read, and writing it is the fatal direction. Charles MacDonald's hardware-tested notes are the source for both, and record the same outcome for both: the machine locks. Neither mistake produces an error you can see, so the symptom is a console that has simply stopped with the last picture still on screen. Worth remembering before pointing anything that dumps a range of memory at the VDP's block, because a read of $C00000-$C0001F passes straight through the PSG.

Work RAM is 64 KB and its canonical address is $FF0000 to $FFFFFF. It holds every variable a game keeps, and it holds the stack: the block of memory a processor uses for return addresses and values it is putting aside for a moment, which on this machine starts at the top of the 64 KB and grows downward as things are pushed onto it. Two true statements about the space just below it answer different questions and should not be merged. Sega's map labels $E00000-$FEFFFF ACCESS PROHIBITED, which is a rule: do not go there. The community documents that the same range answers anyway, because the RAM's address decoding is partial and its 64 KB repeats through the whole range, which is a mirror and a description of behaviour. Both are correct. Use $FF0000, know the mirror exists as an electrical fact, and note that the machine's own documentation forbids relying on it.

Work RAM is at $FF0000, but the game declares it at $FFFF0000 and says so in the comment: "Pretend we're in the RAM". Declaring the high spelling is what lets the assembler use the 68000's short absolute form, whose 16-bit address is sign-extended into the top of the map. The three I/O ports are on the map: $A10003 and $A10005 for the two controllers, $A10007 for the expansion port, each with a direction-control register beside it. And the VDP's ports carry a restriction that has nothing to do with addresses: a whole family of 68000 instructions must never be aimed at them, because of what those instructions do to a port that behaves differently on reads and writes.   • Sixteen megabytes of address space, and almost none of it is memory. An address is a question the whole system bus hears at once, and the map decides which chip answers.
  • Three outcomes, not two: a device answers, or nothing answers and you get open-bus junk that no error ever flags, or nothing completes the bus cycle at all and the 68000 waits forever.

4 · The cartridge

The cartridge is wired into the bottom of the map, $000000 to $3FFFFF: four megabytes of address space, which is the hard ceiling on a plain cartridge without extra hardware on the board to page more in. There is no loading. A Genesis game is not copied anywhere before it runs; the ROM chip is part of the address space, and the 68000 fetches instructions straight out of the plastic. Not only instructions, either. Everything the cartridge shipped with is in there and is read from there: the code, and with it the tile art, the level layouts, the music and sound data, the palettes, and every table and constant the program looks things up in.

A caution about reading official documents, from this exact spot. Sega's own binder states the ROM area three different ways: a front-matter bullet says one megabyte, the memory-map figure draws four ($000000-$3FFFFF), and an appendix advising emulator authors says to enable 512 KB. The figure is the one to trust. The bullet is a snapshot of what cartridges happened to be in 1990, preserved in a document that outlived it.

Because ROM is at the bottom of the map, the first thing the 68000 reads out of reset is the cartridge, on a console without the security system in the way. It begins by reading two longwords from address zero, a longword being four bytes on this processor. The first is the value to load into the stack pointer, the register that holds where the top of section 3's stack currently is; the second is the address of the first instruction to run. Neither is code. Both are data the cartridge supplies, and a Genesis ROM therefore does not begin with a program at all, it begins with a table:

Vectors:
	dc.l System_Stack	; Initial stack pointer value
	dc.l EntryPoint		; Start of program
	dc.l ErrorTrap		; Bus error
	dc.l ErrorTrap		; Address error (4)
s2disasm/s2.asm:85-89

The first eight bytes of the ROM are those first two entries, and the 68000 consumes them before it executes anything. The rest of the table is the vector table: one four-byte slot for each kind of thing that can interrupt or derail the processor, holding the address to jump to when it happens. All four lines above are slots in it: the first two are the boot pair the processor swallows at reset, and the next two, bus error and address error, both point at the same handler, which is what section 5 is about. The 68000 defines 256 slots; a Genesis cartridge uses the first 64, because the ROM header begins immediately after them at $100. Which slot means what, on the 68000's side, is laid out on the programming model page; which two of them the video chip raises, and when, belongs with the frame.

New here? · How does a console know where a game starts?

It does not know, and it never looks. There is no operating system on a Genesis, no loader and no file system, and the 68000 itself parses no header before it starts: what there is instead is an agreement, fixed in the silicon of the processor. Later consoles do read one field of the cartridge before handing over, which is the security system further down this section, but that is a boot ROM's doing and it changes nothing about the processor's own start-up.

When a 68000 is reset it does exactly two things before running anything: it reads four bytes from address 0 into its stack pointer, and four bytes from address 4 into its program counter. Then it starts fetching instructions from wherever that second number pointed. That is the entire start-up procedure.

The rest follows from the map. The cartridge is wired at the bottom of the address space, so address 0 and address 4 are the first eight bytes on the cartridge, so a Genesis ROM's first eight bytes are two addresses the game chose. Plug in a cartridge and the machine falls straight into it. It is also why those first eight bytes cannot be code: the processor has already eaten them as data before it runs a single instruction.

Straight after the vector table comes the header, and it is worth reading as a description of the machine rather than as metadata. Most of these fields are text a person reads and the machine never does. The console-name field on the first line is the exception, and a load-bearing one: on a console with the security system, the boot ROM reads those bytes and refuses to run a cartridge whose first line does not say SEGA:

Note the &$FFFFFF on the two RAM lines. That is the bitwise AND from section 3 again, and here it keeps the bottom 24 bits of a number and throws away everything above them. It is the 24-bit address space again too, and the same fact the ramaddr trick in section 3 was working around, seen from the other end: the game's RAM labels carry the high $FFFF0000 spelling, and the header wants the 24-bit truth, so the extra bits are masked off on the way in.

Board revision One more thing the cartridge may have to do before the machine will cooperate. On consoles from the second hardware revision onward, the video chip stays locked until the program writes the four ASCII letters SEGA to $A14000. Miss it and the next video access locks the machine. Which consoles behave this way is readable from the version register at $A10001, whose low four bits are the hardware revision: revision 1 and up want the letters. This is the mechanism usually called TMSS, and the name is worth pinning to what the documents actually say, because Sega's own manual is a strange witness here.

What Sega's own manual says about it

The check ships as a code listing, LIST 2 Introduced as "the U.S. security software" Reads the version register, masks the low nibble, writes "SEGA" to $A14000 Never uses the word TMSS Never documents $A14000 as a register

So the four letters are first-party documented and the register is not. Sega published the recipe as a piece of code you were expected to copy, in the same shape every shipping game uses, and never wrote down what the address is or what it does. Every description of the register itself comes from the scene taking the machine apart afterwards.

Carry forward
  • The cartridge is wired into the bottom of the map and nothing is ever loaded from it: the 68000 fetches its instructions straight out of the plastic, and its art, level data and tables with them.
  • The first eight bytes of a ROM are data, not code. The processor swallows them at reset as the initial stack pointer and the address of the first instruction, and the rest of the vector table follows behind them.
  • On a console from hardware revision 1 onward, the program has to write the ASCII letters SEGA to $A14000 before it touches the video chip.

5 · Two processors, two worlds

The 68000 runs the game. The Z80 conventionally runs the sound driver, out of its own eight kilobytes of RAM on its own bus. Mostly neither waits for the other, neither can see the other's memory without asking, and most of the time they are genuinely running at once rather than taking turns.

They are not sealed off from each other, because each can reach into the other's world, and that is what the bus arbiter is for. A 68000 that wants to touch the Z80's RAM or the FM chip must first ask for the Z80's bus and wait until the request is granted. Granting is not instantaneous, and there is no fixed number of cycles anyone can quote for it, so code asks and then polls until the answer comes back. The same is true in the other direction: a Z80 fetching a sample out of cartridge ROM is borrowing the 68000's bus, and the 68000 pays for it in stalled cycles.

The registers that drive that handshake, the polling loop everyone writes, the 64 KB world the Z80 sees from its own side, and the sourced story of why sound ended up on this processor are all a subject of their own, with enough material to fill a page, and none of it is settled here. What this page owes you is the shape: two processors, each with memory of its own, one arbiter between them, and no shared program.

The clearest demonstration of that independence is three instructions long and it is in a shipped game. Every unhandled exception in Sonic 2 points at one address: a bus error, an address error, an illegal instruction, a division by zero, an interrupt nobody asked for. All of them arrive here, and this is all that is here:

; ===========================================================================
; Crash/Freeze the 68000. Note that the Z80 continues to run, so the music keeps playing.
; loc_200:
ErrorTrap:
	nop	; delay
	nop	; delay
	bra.s	ErrorTrap	; Loop indefinitely.
s2disasm/s2.asm:185-191

Read the comment first, because it is doing the teaching. "Crash/Freeze the 68000. Note that the Z80 continues to run, so the music keeps playing."

The code does nothing, deliberately. Two nop instructions, the 68000's way of spending time without changing anything, and then a branch back to the label above them. The 68000 is now in a loop it will never leave, and that is the intended behaviour: a game that has hit an error it cannot handle should stop where it is rather than run wild through memory corrupting things on its way out.

What it conspicuously does not do is stop the machine. The Z80 has its own program, in its own RAM, on its own bus, stepped by its own divided clock, and nothing in that three-instruction loop touches any of it. It goes on walking through the music one row at a time and goes on feeding the sound chips. The VDP goes on drawing whatever was last written into its memory. So the observable symptom of a Genesis game hitting an unhandled exception is a frozen picture with the music playing cheerfully on, and anyone who has played enough Mega Drive games has seen exactly that.

It is also worth pinning down how this differs from the hangs in section 3, because they look identical on a television and are not the same failure. Here the 68000 is executing instructions. Something went wrong, the processor took an exception, looked the handler up in the vector table from section 4, jumped there, and is now running a loop as fast as it can. A DTACK hang is one level below that: the processor never completes the bus cycle at all, so it is not executing anything, and no exception is taken because nothing ever tells it the access failed. There is no timeout in the chip and nothing on the board reports the failure, so as far as the 68000 knows it is still waiting for a slow device. One is a program that has given up. The other is a processor that has stopped being a processor.

New here? · The game has crashed. Why is there still music?

Because on this machine "the game" and "the music" are two different computers, and only one of them crashed.

The mental model most people arrive with is a single brain that does everything, so when the picture freezes the sound should stop too, the way a stalled program stops producing all of its output at once. That model is right for most computers you have used and wrong for this one.

The Z80 is not a subroutine the 68000 calls. It is a processor with its own copy of a program, sitting in memory the 68000 cannot see without asking permission, stepping through that program on its own clock. Nothing about the 68000 being stuck reaches it, unless the 68000 happened to stop while it was holding the Z80's bus, in which case the music stops with it. It does not check on the 68000; it has no way to and no reason to. It just carries on with the driver it was given, which is a loop that reads the next note out of a table and writes it to a sound chip.

The same reasoning explains the other half of what you see. The picture freezes rather than going black because the VDP is a third independent thing: it redraws the screen from its own memory sixty times a second, and it will keep redrawing the last thing anybody wrote there indefinitely. A frozen Genesis is three chips, two of which are still working perfectly.

Carry forward
  • The two processors mostly run at the same time. Either one can reach into the other's world, but only by asking the arbiter for the bus and waiting an amount of time nobody can quote.
  • A 68000 that stops does not stop the Z80 or the VDP, which is why a dead Genesis usually shows a frozen picture with the music still playing.
  • A crash and a hang are different failures. A crash is a program stuck in a loop it chose; a hang is a bus cycle that never completes, with no exception taken and no way back.

6 · The VDP and its three memories

The video chip owns three separate memories, and not one of them appears anywhere on the sixteen-megabyte map in section 3.

VRAM is the big one: 64 kilobytes, and the only general-purpose store of the three. Four things live in it, and each is worth a sentence rather than a name.

Tile art is the bulk of it. A tile is a small square image, eight pixels by eight, and everything the VDP draws is built out of them. The nametables are the grids that place those tiles: a plane is one of the scrolling layers of the picture, a cell is one 8x8 square of that layer, and the nametable is the list saying which tile goes in which cell. The sprite table is the list of every sprite, which is an image the chip moves around freely instead of locking to a grid: Sonic and the badniks rather than the scenery. And the horizontal scroll table holds the sideways scroll values the chip reads as it works its way down the picture, which is what lets one layer slide past another.

Where each of those sits inside the 64 KB is not fixed by the hardware. The program tells the VDP where it put them, which means a Genesis game's video memory layout is a budget somebody chose.

CRAM is the colour memory: 64 slots arranged as four palettes of sixteen, each slot holding a nine-bit colour, and all 64 of them together are 128 bytes. Every colour on screen at any instant comes out of those 64 entries, and the one thing that stretches the count is the chip's shadow and highlight mode, which can show an entry darkened or brightened as well as plain. That is the constraint that gives Genesis pictures their particular look.

VSRAM is the smallest: 80 bytes of vertical scroll values, one 16-bit entry each, so forty of them. Two entries would be enough to scroll two whole planes up and down; the rest are there so that columns of the picture can scroll by different amounts, which is where a lot of the depth in a Mega Drive background comes from.

None of the three can be addressed by the 68000, and this is the fact that shapes everything a program does with graphics. There is no address in the map that is a byte of VRAM. What exists instead is a keyhole: two ports in the 68000's address space, a data port at $C00000 and a control port at $C00004, through which the processor first writes a command saying which of the three memories it wants and at what address, and then moves the bytes through the data port. Bulk transfers go the same way but are driven by the VDP itself rather than by the CPU, which is what DMA is. The three memories and the keyhole model are the opening of the VDP page, and the two ports and the exact shape of that command word are its section on the ports.

New here? · Why can the CPU not just write to video memory?

Because the video chip is reading that memory almost all the time, and it cannot be interrupted while it does.

Think about what the VDP has to do. Every scanline of the picture, sixty times a second, it must fetch nametable entries, then the tiles they name, then the sprites that overlap that line, then the colours for every pixel it produces. That is a lot of reading, on a schedule set by the television, and being late is not an option because the electron beam does not wait.

If the 68000 shared those wires, every access it made would be a chip that owns a deadline being told to wait its turn. So it does not share them. VRAM sits on the VDP's own private bus, and the only way in is to hand the VDP an address and a value and let it do the write itself, when it has a spare moment.

That is what the two ports are: not a window onto video memory, but a request desk. It is also why the phrase "you can only update graphics during the blanking interval" turns up everywhere. When the beam is drawing, the VDP has very few spare moments; between frames, it has nothing else to do at all.

The VDP also keeps the machine's time. It raises an interrupt when it finishes the visible picture, and it can raise a second one at a point part-way down the picture that the program controls. Those two interrupts are how a game stays in step with the display: they are the reason game code runs sixty times a second rather than as fast as it can. The window the first one opens, between one frame and the next, is called VBlank, and it is the only comfortable time for bulk video updates. The scanline-by-scanline machinery, the two interrupts and exactly what fits in that window are the frame's own subject. For this page it is enough to know that the part which draws the picture is also the part that keeps the beat.

Carry forward
  • Three memories, none of them on the 68000's map: VRAM at 64 kilobytes, CRAM at 128 bytes, VSRAM at 80.
  • Everything reaches them through a keyhole. A command goes to the control port at $C00004 saying which memory and which address, and then the bytes go through the data port at $C00000, either pushed by the CPU or copied by DMA.
  • The VDP also keeps the machine's time. It raises an interrupt when the visible picture ends, and the window that opens there is when bulk video work is comfortable.

7 · A tour of one frame

NTSC / PAL Put those five headline functions and their glue together and run them for one frame, which is just under a sixtieth of a second on an NTSC console and just under a fiftieth on a PAL one. Nothing in this section is new. It is the same five functions on the same three buses, all moving at once.

The VDP is drawing. It walks down the picture one scanline at a time, a picture 320 pixels wide, or 256 in the narrower mode the chip also offers. For each line it reads nametable entries out of VRAM over its own private video bus, fetches the tiles those entries name, looks each pixel's colour up in CRAM, and mixes in whichever sprites overlap that line. The 68000 is not involved, and nothing requires it to watch: all it can see of the beam is the H/V counter at $C00008, if it goes looking. How a scrolling plane is built out of nametable words, what happens when two things want the same pixel, and how the window takes over a rectangle of the screen are the video chip's own subject: the VDP and planes covers all of it, including the priority bit that decides which of two layers a cell belongs to. Tiles and the VRAM budget, colour and CRAM, and the sprite table with its per-line limits are the pages after that.

Meanwhile the 68000 is running the game, fetching its instructions straight out of the cartridge at the bottom of the map and keeping every value it changes in work RAM at the top. ROM holds what the cartridge shipped with and never changes: the code, and the art, the data and the constants alongside it. The state that moves while the game runs is somewhere else, and not in one place either, but spread across work RAM here, the Z80's eight kilobytes, and the VDP's own three memories. Work RAM is where the physics, the object logic and the collision happen. What the 68000 mostly cannot do right now is push data at the video chip, because the VDP is busy reading VRAM for the picture and has almost no spare moments to service requests. So the frame's video work piles up in RAM waiting for a window.

Meanwhile the Z80 is running the sound driver, out of its own eight kilobytes, on its own bus, at one fifteenth of the same crystal. It walks its music data one row at a time and writes the YM2612 directly, because the FM chip is on its bus. When it wants the PSG it has to reach across the arbiter into the 68000's world instead, because the PSG is inside the VDP: section 1's asymmetry, showing up as two different pieces of code in the same driver. If it is playing a recorded sample it is also fetching bytes out of cartridge ROM, which means borrowing the 68000's bus and slowing the 68000 down a little every time.

Then the last visible line is drawn and the VDP raises its interrupt. The 68000 drops whatever it was doing and jumps to the handler whose address is sitting in the vector table from section 4. VBlank has opened: for a short window the VDP is not drawing, so its memories are finally available at full speed. This is when the frame's video work actually happens. The new sprite table goes out, the new scroll values, any changed palette entries, any new art. The bulk of it is not copied by the processor at all but by DMA, where the VDP is told a source in 68000 memory and a length and does the copying itself, considerably faster than the CPU could, and holding the 68000 still while it works. That is why a Genesis game's entire graphics budget is really a measurement of what fits in this window, and it is why the window's size, the cost of a transfer, and the queue that decides what gets sent first are a topic in their own right, along with the two interrupts and the scanline counting behind them.

Then the beam returns to the top and it all starts again, sixty times a second on an NTSC machine and fifty on a PAL one, for as long as the console is on. The Z80 noticed almost none of it: apart from the moments the two processors borrowed each other's buses, it has been keeping its own time from the same crystal the whole way through.

That is the machine. Everything after this page takes one piece of it and opens it up: the video chip and its planes first, then tiles and VRAM, colour and CRAM, sprites, DMA, the frame and its interrupts, the two processors and the bus between them, and the two sound chips.

Carry forward · into the rest of the track
  • Every part is working at once inside a single frame: the VDP walking down the picture, the 68000 running the game out of ROM into work RAM, and the Z80 stepping the music, all of them counting from one crystal.
  • Video work waits for the gap between frames, so a game's graphics budget is really a measurement of what fits in that window.
  • The parts hold each other up only where they touch: a borrowed bus, a DMA that pauses the 68000, an interrupt that lands on the beat.
