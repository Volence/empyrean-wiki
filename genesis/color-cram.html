The Genesis

Color & CRAM

A Genesis pixel is a number from 0 to 15, and a number is not a colour. What turns it into light is a small memory inside the video chip holding sixty-four colour slots, nine bits each, written blue first. This page is that memory: the nine-bit word and its bit layout, the four palettes and who chooses between them, why eight steps per channel is the whole reason Genesis skies come out in stripes, the shadow and highlight mode in full, and what happens when a game rewrites its colours halfway down the screen.

This page picks up where Tiles & VRAM leaves off: a pixel is four bits of palette index, and nothing on screen has a colour until CRAM does. Two things it does not re-teach, because The VDP & Planes owns them: how a value reaches the chip at all (two ports and one command word), and what the priority bit does (one bit, seven layers). Section 5 needs both, and links rather than repeats.

1 · Sixty-four slots

CRAM is the smallest and least flexible of the three memories inside the VDP, and it does exactly one job. It is 128 bytes, addressed as 64 words of 16 bits, and it holds 64 colours. That is the whole of it. There is no spare corner of CRAM to put something else in, and no way to trade colour slots for extra room.

The interesting part is the gap between how CRAM is addressed and what it stores. Sega's own description is "64 9 bit words inside the VDP chip": each slot is reached as a 16-bit word, and only nine of those sixteen bits exist. Seven bits of every word are simply not implemented. Those positions do not store anything, so they are not seven spare flags per colour waiting to be used.

New here? · What does "nine bits in a sixteen-bit word" mean?

A memory is usually described by two numbers that people expect to match: how many boxes there are, and how big a box is. CRAM has 64 boxes, and each box is reached as though it were 16 bits wide, because that is the size the chip's data port moves. But only nine of those sixteen positions have anything behind them. The other seven are wiring that was never put in.

Think of a form with sixteen boxes printed on it where only nine are actually read by anybody. You can write in the other seven. Nothing will come of it. The form is not lying to you and nothing is broken; the boxes are printed because the paper comes that size.

Two practical consequences follow, and both turn up later on this page. A colour is written as a whole word rather than as a byte, because the word is the unit. And the seven unimplemented positions are not spare storage: if you need somewhere to keep a flag per colour, it goes in your own RAM, not in the top of a CRAM entry.

That is also why CRAM is written a whole word at a time. A colour is one word, the word is the unit the VDP stores, and the two halves of it are not independently meaningful.

New here? · Why do the colours need a memory of their own?

Because the picture does not carry its colours around with it. A tile is an 8 by 8 square of pixels, and each pixel is four bits: a number from 0 to 15. Not a colour. Just a number, meaning "the ninth colour", or "the second colour", with no statement anywhere in the tile about what the ninth colour actually looks like.

So something has to hold the answer, and that something is CRAM. Sixty-four slots, and every pixel on screen is a small number pointing at one of them. Rewrite a slot and every pixel pointing at it changes colour on the next line the chip draws, without a single pixel of art being touched. That one indirection is the source of nearly every colour trick on the machine, including the whole of sections 4, 5 and 6 below.

The cost of the trick is the ceiling. Sixty-four slots is sixty-four, and it is not negotiable the way video memory is. When a game is short of art space it can compress, share tiles, or reload mid-level. When it is short of colours it has to want fewer colours.

CRAM is fixed in size and fixed in purpose, and this is the point where it stops resembling the memory next door. VRAM is 65,536 bytes of general store whose contents and layout are entirely the programmer's problem: art, plane maps, the sprite list, all of it wherever the game decides to put it. CRAM has no layout decisions in it at all. Slot 0 is slot 0.

The order is palette-major, which is the one arrangement fact worth memorising: entry n is colour n mod 16 of palette n div 16. Entries 0 to 15 are palette 0, entries 16 to 31 are palette 1, and so on. A palette line is therefore 16 words, which is 32 bytes, so palette p begins at CRAM byte address p times 32. Sonic 2 keeps the size as a named constant rather than a magic number:

palette_line_size =		$10*2	; 16 word entries
s2disasm/s2.constants.asm:1042

Getting a colour into a slot uses the same keyhole as everything else the VDP owns: set an address through the control port, then write words to the data port, with the auto-increment walking the address forward for you. That machinery belongs to The VDP & Planes, section 2, and this page assumes it. What it looks like in practice is four lines, and Sonic 2's checksum failure screen is the smallest complete example in either disassembly:

	move.l	#vdpComm($0000,CRAM,WRITE),(VDP_control_port).l ; set VDP to CRAM write
	moveq	#16*4-1,d7 ; all colours of all palette lines
; loc_3E2:
Checksum_Red:
	move.w	#$00E,(VDP_data_port).l	; fill palette with red
	dbf	d7,Checksum_Red		; repeat $3F more times
s2disasm/s2.asm:450-455 (ChecksumError, the boot-time checksum failure screen)

Point the VDP at CRAM address zero, then write the same word sixty-four times. The loop counter is spelled 16*4-1, which is four palettes of sixteen written out as arithmetic, and $00E is a colour you will be able to read by the end of section 2. This runs only when the cartridge fails its own checksum at boot, so nobody has seen it in normal play; it is quoted because it is CRAM at its most naked, with no palette buffer, no fade and no queue in the way.

2 · The nine-bit word, and it is BGR

Nine bits, three per channel, three channels. Each channel gets a value from 0 to 7, so the machine can express 8 x 8 x 8 = 512 colours, and CRAM has 64 slots to hold a selection of them in.

The order is blue, green, red. Blue occupies the most significant of the three fields, not the least. Almost everybody guesses the other way round on first contact, because "RGB" is the phrase in everyone's head, and the guess costs an afternoon of wondering why the sky is orange. Written as three hex digits the word reads $BGR: $00E is full red and $E00 is full blue.

Look at where a channel sits inside its nibble and the second surprise falls out. The three bits are not at the bottom of the nibble; they are one place up. So the nibble's value is always twice the channel level, which means a channel's hex digit is always one of 0 2 4 6 8 A C E. There are no odd digits in a legal colour. An odd digit in a palette entry is a bug, not a shade the reader has not met yet, and it is one of the easiest mistakes to spot by eye.

New here? · How do three small numbers make a colour?

The same way a stage light does. Point a red lamp, a green lamp and a blue lamp at one spot and turn each up or down, and between them they can make most colours a person can see. All three off is black. All three full is white. Red full with the others off is red. Red and green full together, no blue, is yellow, which surprises people the first time.

A Genesis has the same three lamps and a dimmer switch with only eight positions on it: off, then seven brightnesses. Three numbers from 0 to 7 is the entire description of a colour on this machine, and the colour word is just those three numbers parked in one 16-bit value.

Eight positions per lamp is the thing to hold on to, because section 4 is about what happens when eight is not enough.

The clearest statement of the format in either disassembly is not a table. It is a fade routine, and it states the layout as arithmetic. Sonic 2 fades a screen in by walking each colour one step towards its target, and to do that it has to know how far one step is in each channel:

;.updateblue:
	move.w	d3,d1
	addi.w	#$200,d1	; increase blue value
	cmp.w	d2,d1		; has blue reached threshold level?
	bhi.s	.updategreen	; if yes, branch
	move.w	d1,(a0)+	; update palette
	rts

; loc_2454: Pal_AddGreen:
.updategreen:
	move.w	d3,d1
	addi.w	#$20,d1		; increase green value
	cmp.w	d2,d1
	bhi.s	.updatered
	move.w	d1,(a0)+	; update palette
	rts

; loc_2462: Pal_AddRed:
.updatered:
	addq.w	#2,(a0)+	; increase red value
	rts
s2disasm/s2.asm:3331-3351 (the per-colour step of Pal_FadeFromBlack, the fade-in at every screen transition)

Three constants, and they are the format. One step of blue is $200, because blue starts at bit 9. One step of green is $20, because green starts at bit 5. One step of red is 2, because red starts at bit 1 and not bit 0. A reader who did not believe the diagram above can derive it from these three numbers, and this code has been in a shipped cartridge since 1992.

The routine brightens whichever channel it can and returns, which is why a Genesis fade-in crawls up through blue first, then green, then red: it is walking the word's own fields in order, not mixing a colour.

3 · Four palettes of sixteen, and who picks

The 64 slots are not one flat list as far as the drawing hardware is concerned. They are four palettes of sixteen, and a pixel's four bits index one of those sixteen. Four bits cannot reach past sixteen, so the pixel value alone can never say which palette it means.

Which produces the load-bearing inversion of this whole page. A tile carries no palette information at all. The 32 bytes of a tile are indices and nothing else. The palette is chosen by whatever is REFERENCING the tile, and the two referencing structures choose it differently:

  • For a plane cell, bits 14 and 13 of the nametable word. The palette is a property of the cell, so the same tile drawn in two cells of the same plane can wear two different palettes.
  • For a sprite, two bits of the sprite's attribute word, applying to the whole sprite at once rather than per tile. A sprite is one palette from end to end, however many tiles it is built from.

The consequence is worth spelling out because it is the reason palettes are a technique and not just a storage format: the same tile art draws in four colour schemes for nothing, and recolouring a whole scene costs the 32 bytes of one palette line where redrawing it would cost the art. Every "same enemy, different zone" sprite you have ever noticed on this machine is that arithmetic.

New here? · Why can two games share tiles and look different?

Picture a colouring book printed with numbers in the shapes rather than colours. The printed page says 1 here, 4 there, 9 over there. It does not say what 4 is. Hand the same printed page to two people with different sets of pencils and you get two different pictures out of one drawing.

A tile is that printed page and a palette is the set of pencils. The pixels say 1, 4 and 9; CRAM says what 1, 4 and 9 look like. So a game that wants an autumn version of a forest does not redraw a single leaf. It writes sixteen new words into CRAM and the forest changes while the art in video memory sits untouched.

It also explains a thing that trips people reading a disassembly for the first time: the palette files and the art files are separate, and a palette is tiny. Thirty-two bytes buys the recolour of an entire screen, which is why games change palettes constantly and change art as rarely as they can get away with.

The colour index that is not a colour

Index 0 of a palette is special, and its speciality is a drawing rule rather than a property of the slot. In a plane cell or in a sprite, a pixel whose index is 0 is not drawn. Whatever colour sits in that palette's slot 0 has no effect on it: the pixel is transparent, and what shows through is whatever is behind. So a tile shows at most 15 of its palette's 16 entries, however carefully its slot 0 was filled in.

New here? · If index 0 is invisible, why does a palette have one?

Because the transparency is a rule about DRAWING, not a property of the slot, and the two are easy to run together. The slot is an ordinary CRAM entry holding an ordinary colour word. What is special is that when the chip is drawing a plane cell or a sprite and meets a pixel whose index is 0, it does not draw a pixel at all. It leaves whatever was already there.

So the slot has to exist, because a palette is sixteen entries and the pixel values run 0 to 15; index 0 has to be somewhere in that run, and starting at 0 is what four bits do. It is simply the one index every tile agrees to spend on "nothing here".

And the colour in it is not entirely wasted. One register, the backdrop below, can point at any slot in CRAM including a slot 0, and whatever it points at is what you see where nothing else is drawn. That is why the arithmetic in the next paragraph loses four slots and gets exactly one of them back.

Now count what is actually visible. Four palettes, four slot-0 entries, each of which no plane or sprite pixel can ever show: that is four of the 64 slots spent. One of them comes back, and exactly one, through the backdrop register below, which can point at any slot including a slot 0. So:

64 slots - 4 unshowable slot-0 entries + 1 of them restored as the backdrop = 61

Sixty-one is therefore the most distinct CRAM ENTRIES a picture built from planes and sprites can show at one instant, and it is a ceiling rather than a typical figure: a game that points its backdrop at an ordinary colour it is already using gets 60. The 64 that specification sheets quote is the count of SLOTS, which is a different question with a true answer of its own. Be careful what the 61 is counting, because the two sections after this one both move colours around without touching it: section 5 puts those same 61 entries on screen in three brightness states at once, and section 6 changes which 61 they are partway down the frame. Neither adds a slot to CRAM.

The backdrop register

Wherever no plane pixel and no sprite pixel is drawn, the VDP shows the backdrop, and one register says which slot the backdrop reads. It is VDP register 7, written through the control port as $87xx, and its low byte carries two fields:

The backdrop can be any of the 64 entries, not just entry 0. The widely taught version, "the backdrop is palette 0 colour 0", describes the common default and not the register. Both Sonic games contradict it in shipped code. Sonic 2 sets palette 2 on entering a Zone:

	move.w	#$8720,(a6)		; Background palette/color: 2/0
	move.w	#$8C81,(a6)		; H res 40 cells, no interlace
s2disasm/s2.asm:4833-4834 (Level_InitWater and the shared level VDP setup)

and Sonic 3 and Knuckles sets palette 3 for its Competition menu, with $8730 at skdisasm/sonic3k.asm:13454. Worth being exact about what those two prove, since they are the only shipped evidence to hand: both games exercise the PALETTE half of the field and leave the colour half at 0. Across the whole of both disassemblies the only backdrop values written are $8700, $8720 and $8730. The colour bits are real, documented, and apparently something neither game needed.

What a palette actually looks like

It looks like sixteen words, and that is genuinely all. There is no header, no count, no terminator. Palette data in the Sonic disassemblies ships as binary files rather than as assembly, so there is no shipped source listing to quote here; the example below is invented for the page, and it is invented rather than dumped so that every entry can be one you can read:

Teaching example · not from a shipped game 
; one palette line: 16 words, 32 bytes, written blue-green-red
Pal_Example:
	dc.w	$000	; 0  never drawn in a tile or a sprite
	dc.w	$000	; 1  black, for outlines
	dc.w	$222	; 2  near-black
	dc.w	$444	; 3  dark grey
	dc.w	$668	; 4  grey, slightly warm
	dc.w	$88A	; 5
	dc.w	$AAC	; 6
	dc.w	$CCE	; 7
	dc.w	$EEE	; 8  white
	dc.w	$008	; 9  dark red   (blue 0, green 0, red 4)
	dc.w	$00E	; 10 red        (red at full)
	dc.w	$06E	; 11 orange     (red full, some green)
	dc.w	$0EE	; 12 yellow     (red and green full)
	dc.w	$080	; 13 dark green
	dc.w	$E00	; 14 blue       (blue at full)
	dc.w	$E88	; 15 pale blue

Every digit is even, which is the section 2 rule showing up in real data. Entry 0 is written as black out of habit and it makes no difference what it holds, because no tile pixel and no sprite pixel will ever show it. The one thing that can is the backdrop register, if it is pointed here.

Gotcha Two palettes of sixteen is not the same as one palette of thirty-two. A pixel's four bits choose within a line and cannot cross out of it, and the palette is chosen per cell or per sprite, never per pixel. So a single tile cannot show eighteen colours, no matter how many free slots CRAM has. Eighteen colours inside one 8x8 square of screen means more than one thing drawn there: two tiles in two cells, or a sprite laid over a plane cell, each still within its own sixteen.

4 · The ramp, and why the Genesis bands

Everything so far has been about what the colour word ENCODES. This section is about what comes out of the console, and the two are not the same shape.

What the word encodes is regular: eight levels per channel, evenly numbered 0 to 7. What the video output does with those numbers is not evenly spaced. The ramp is not linear. The steps are widest at the two ends and compressed through the middle. That is the fact, it holds on every Mega Drive anyone has put an instrument on, and the two independent measurements of it agree about that shape even where they disagree about the digits.

The numbers themselves are one measurement chain and this page treats them as one. The set below was measured by TmEE with an oscilloscope on the console's own video output, and it is the set reproduced almost everywhere the ramp is quoted. Scaled so that the top of the ramp is 255, one channel's eight normal-mode levels come out as:

0   52   87   116   144   172   206   255

A second, genuinely independent measurement, taken from voltages by Eke and worked up by Mask of Destiny, the author of BlastEm, reads 0 49 87 119 146 174 206 255: two or three units away at four of those stops and identical at the other four. Neither is the answer, and averaging them would manufacture a third number nobody measured. Mask of Destiny puts the spread down to small differences between individual DACs, which means the last unit or two is a property of the console in front of you rather than a figure still waiting to be pinned down. The shape is the fact; the digits are one chain's reading of it.

Take the differences between those eight stops and the shape becomes arithmetic. The gaps are 52, 35, 29, 28, 28, 34, 49, which is a wide step at the bottom, four narrow ones through the middle, and a wide one at the top. Two teaching points fall straight out, and the first of them is the opposite of what most people assume.

Banding is worst in the deep shadows and near white, and mildest in the midtones. The step from level 0 to level 1 is 52 out of 255, nearly twice the size of the steps through the middle of the range. So a dark gradient, which is exactly what a night sky or a cave wall is, has the coarsest staircase available. Artists who lit their dark areas up a little were not being timid; they were climbing out of the widest part of the ladder.

And every step is huge. The smallest gap here is 28 out of 255, about 11% of full scale, and the largest is 20%. On a large flat area a human eye picks out brightness differences well under 1%. So a large gradient built from eight levels cannot be smooth on its own: the seams are more than ten times wider than the threshold at which they stop being invisible. That is the entire explanation of Genesis banding, and it is why dithering, checkerboarding two levels together to fake a third, is everywhere on this machine.

New here? · Why does a Genesis sky come out in stripes?

Because a sky wants to fade and the machine can only step. Think of a paint mixer with eight cans of blue and no way to blend them. To paint a sky that gets lighter towards the horizon you use can 1 at the top, then can 2, then can 3, and each change of can is a visible line across your sky. Nothing is broken. There are only eight cans.

Modern hardware has 256 cans per channel and the steps between them are below what your eye can pick out, so a gradient looks continuous. The Genesis has eight, and the gaps between them are enormous by comparison, so you see every one.

The trick artists used is dithering: instead of a solid band of can 2 next to a solid band of can 3, checkerboard the two together in the region between. Up close it is a chequerboard. On a television of the period, which smeared neighbouring pixels together anyway, it read as a shade in between. That is why so much Genesis art looks speckled when you finally see it on a sharp modern screen; the speckles were doing a job the palette could not.

Why the ramp is bent at all is a question about the silicon, and only one source ventures an answer. plutiedev reports that the VDP die shows each channel's converter built as a resistor chain with evenly spaced taps, and suggests, hedging with a "seems", that the designers did not account for analog effects that distort the values on the way out. If that is right the curve is an accident rather than a deliberate gamma choice. It is one source and it hedges, so it is offered here the same way.

Gotcha Do not read a doubled hex digit as a measurement. This wiki writes a Genesis colour on screen by repeating its CRAM digit, so $EEE is drawn as #EEEEEE. That is a notation for the stored value, chosen because it stays inside the console's own eight levels, and it is what Genesis Plus GX ships as well. It is not a claim about the light: the measured top of the ramp is 255 and the ladder below it is bent. The console's $EEE is the only white it has, and how bright that white is belongs to the console, the cable and the display, not to a hex string.

One more thing before section 5, because it is the door into it. Everything above describes the ramp the chip uses NORMALLY. There is a mode in which each channel can land on fifteen distinct levels instead of eight, and those fifteen are not a finer ramp. They are the same ladder, read in three overlapping slices of eight.

5 · Shadow and highlight

The VDP has one mode that changes what a colour word means, and it is described wrongly more often than anything else on the chip. Accounts in the wild get the enable wrong, collapse three separate routes into one, drop the precondition that makes it work, or invent a bit in CRAM that does not exist. This section is long because each of those is worth dismantling by name.

The enable

One bit turns the whole thing on: bit 3 of VDP register 12, which Sega's register table names S/TE, documenting it exactly as "1: Enable SHADOW and HIGHLIGHT. 0: Disable SHADOW and HIGHLIGHT". It is a global switch, not a per-cell or per-colour property. With it clear, everything below this heading stops existing.

Sonic 2 writes both values in the same block of level setup, which makes the bit position readable without a manual: $8C81 for off, $8C89 for on, a difference of exactly $08, which is bit 3.

Naming where the bit lives also disposes of the fourth misconception in one line, so it can be dropped here rather than argued with later. There is no bit in CRAM that marks a colour as shadowed or highlighted. A CRAM entry is nine bits of colour in a 16-bit word and section 2 accounts for every one of them. The switch is in a VDP register and the operators are ordinary sprites, so what a colour word MEANS is decided entirely outside the memory that holds it.

What it does to a colour

With the mode on, every pixel on screen ends up in exactly one of three states: normal, shadowed, or highlighted. Not a range, not a blend, not a per-pixel amount. Three states.

The three states read the same fifteen-rung ladder from section 4 in three overlapping slices, and TmEE, who measured it directly, is blunt about the count: "there's only 15 levels", adding that shadow and highlight introduce no new ones, "not even subtly different ones". This matters because it decides two behaviours people find counter-intuitive:

  • Highlighting white does nothing. A channel already at level 7 reads 255 normal and 255 highlighted. There is no rung above it. Emulators used to get this wrong by assuming a sixteenth level and producing a whiter white than any console makes.
  • Shadowing black does nothing. Level 0 reads 0 in both. There is no rung below it either.

How many colours the mode can express is a question worth answering carefully, because the usual answer is wrong in an interesting way. A pixel is normal, shadowed or highlighted as a whole, so the expressible set is three 512-colour cubes laid over each other, and they overlap because the slices overlap. Multiplying gives 512 x 3 = 1536, which double counts every colour that two slices share. Working the overlaps out properly, the honest figure is about 1400. The exact count is in the refbox below, labelled as our own arithmetic rather than a published number, because it is.

Reference · Where "about 1400" comes from

Derived here from the level table in section 4, not quoted from a source. Per channel, the normal and shadow slices share four rungs (0, 52, 87, 116); normal and highlight share four (144, 172, 206, 255); shadow and highlight share one (130); no rung is in all three. Cubing those and applying inclusion-exclusion over the three 512-colour cubes:

3 x 512 - (4 x 4 x 4) - (4 x 4 x 4) - (1 x 1 x 1) = 1536 - 129 = 1407

Which is why the prose says "about 1400" and not a crisp figure: the input is one measurement chain, the arithmetic is ours, and nobody publishes the number. The load-bearing part is the correction to 1536, which is the number you will see quoted and which counts the shared colours twice.

Three mechanisms, not one

Here is the part descriptions get wrong most often. With S/TE set, there are THREE separate routes into those states, and the first has nothing to do with the other two. One is about plane priority. Two are about particular colour indices used by sprites.

Priority

If both plane A and plane B are low priority at a pixel, that pixel is shadowed. This is Sega's own statement of the rule and three modern sources restate it. A low-priority sprite drawn over such a region is shadowed along with it; a high-priority sprite is not.
Colour 15

A sprite pixel using palette 3, colour 15 is a SHADOW OPERATOR. Sega's wording is that these dots "work as an operator" and so are not displayed: the sprite's own pixel is not drawn at all, and what lies underneath it becomes shadowed.
Colour 14

A sprite pixel using palette 3, colour 14 is a HIGHLIGHT OPERATOR. Same mechanism, opposite direction: the pixel is not drawn and what is under it brightens.

Kabuto's hardware notes name the two operators the precise way, by CRAM index rather than by palette and colour: entry 63 shadows and entry 62 highlights, which is the same thing said without leaving room for "colour 15 of which palette". His summary of the second one is worth having in his own words, because it contains the cancellation rule as well: sprite colour 62 is "not drawn and the A/B/G color becomes highlighted if it was normal and normal if it was shadowed".

New here? · What is an operator sprite?

An ordinary sprite is a picture: you give it tiles and it draws them over the background. An operator sprite is not a picture at all. It is a stencil that says "darken what is behind me" or "brighten what is behind me", and it draws nothing of its own.

The way the hardware expresses that is a little startling the first time: there is no "operator" flag anywhere. You just build a sprite out of one particular colour index, and the chip treats those pixels as instructions instead of as colour. Palette 3 colour 15 darkens. Palette 3 colour 14 brightens. Everything else in the same sprite draws normally, so a sprite can be a picture in most of its pixels and a stencil in the rest.

The cost is that those two slots of palette 3 stop being usable as colours in sprites, and a game either accepts that or leaves the mode off. Which, as the end of this section notes, most games did.

The rules that get stated wrong

The operator sprite has to actually be on top. This precondition is left out of most short explanations, and without it the effect silently does not happen. Two independent sources state it: rasterscroll puts it as the sprite needing to be at the top of the display with no higher-priority plane above it, and Kabuto phrases the same thing as the operator working only where the sprite "would normally be on top". A low-priority operator under a high-priority plane A cell does nothing at all.

Shadow does not stack. A shadow operator over an already shadowed region leaves it shadowed and no darker. There is one shadow state, not a depth of shadow.

Highlight cancels shadow cleanly. Bright over dark gives you back a normal pixel rather than some muddle in between, which is the behaviour that makes the two operators composable at all.

The operators do not affect other sprites. They reach the planes and the backdrop under them. It is why a spotlight built from operator sprites, as Mega Turrican's is, passes across the player without changing him. Keep this distinct from the priority mechanism at the top of the list, which does shadow a low-priority sprite: what is exempt from the OPERATORS is not exempt from the all-low-priority rule.

Gotcha Colour index 14 refuses to be shadowed in palettes 0, 1 and 2 as well, not only where the operators live. A sprite pixel whose colour index is 14, whether from palette 0, 1 or 2, draws at normal brightness even when everything around it is shadowed. Kabuto states the underlying rule as a bit test rather than as a special case, and it is the most precise version anywhere: if the low four bits of the sprite's colour index are 14, shadow is disabled for that pixel. That is CRAM entries 14, 30 and 46, plus 62, which is already spoken for as the highlight operator. plutiedev's advice is to stop fighting it and use it: make colour 14 the light that stays lit when everything else goes dark.

The priority interaction, which is where the misconceptions come from

The mode leans on the priority bit that The VDP & Planes owns, and it leans on it in a way that catches people out. A high-priority plane cell forces its whole 8x8 area to normal brightness, and it does so whether or not it contains any opaque pixels at all. A transparent pixel in a plane still takes part in the priority evaluation that decides shadowing. A transparent pixel in a SPRITE does not. That asymmetry is not decoration; it is a technique.

Ranger-X is the clearest worked example, and rasterscroll demonstrated it from footage by toggling priority in an emulator. The game runs a whole level with its background shadowed. When the player opens a hole in the ceiling, the tiles in the hole are transparent and are flipped to high priority, which cancels the shadow in that area and lets the normal-brightness picture show through. The result looks exactly like a shaft of light, and no highlight operator was involved. Two other titles rasterscroll analyses in the same way: Battle Mania 2 builds a window reflection by giving the reflection sprites low priority so they shadow while the player's identical tiles stay high priority, and Mega Turrican reaches for the mode twice in one game by two different routes: its water surface toggles the enable bit itself partway down the frame with a raster effect, and its spotlight is built from palette-3 colour-14 sprites. plutiedev, with no second source, additionally names Castlevania Bloodlines, Bari-Arm and Vectorman for light-ray and glass effects of the same family.

What the Sonic games actually do, which is not the folklore

Two corrections, both readable in the disassemblies, and both the opposite of what gets repeated.

No Sonic 2 or Sonic 3 and Knuckles Zone uses shadow and highlight. Every shipped Zone leaves bit 3 clear: $8C81 normally, $8C87 for the two-player split screen. But Sonic 2 does turn the mode on in one place every player reaches, the half-pipe Special Stage:

	move.w	#$8C08,(a6)		; H res 32 cells, no interlace, S/H enabled

The only other enable in the game is behind a cheat:

	beq.s	++
	btst	#button_C,(Ctrl_1_Held).w
	beq.s	+

Normal play takes the first line and stops. Holding C with the debug options flag set takes the second, which is a switch left in the ROM rather than a feature of the game.

Sonic 3 and Knuckles ships the mode on in five more scenes. None of them is a Zone, and none of the five writes carries a comment, which is precisely why the technique is usually recorded as unused. A text search of that file for the word "shadow" turns up the boot table's note that the mode is OFF, Special Stage shadow art and the routine that draws it, and a Z80 comment, and not one of the five places the mode is switched on. How that list was found is worth as much as the list. Searching a disassembly for the English word "shadow" finds the comments somebody happened to write, which is why the mode reads as unused: not one of the six enables above carries the word. Searching for the register VALUE $8C89 is much better and finds all five of the Sonic 3 and Knuckles writes at once. But $8C89 is exactly what misses Sonic 2's Special Stage, because that scene runs in 32-cell mode and writes $8C08: the same bit 3, set alongside different neighbours in the same register. The sweep that finds every enable in both games is over the FIELD, not over a literal: any write to register 12 whose low byte has bit 3 set. Grepping the value beats grepping the prose, and grepping the field beats grepping any one value.

Worth noticing that Sonic 3 and Knuckles' Special Stage does both: it turns the hardware mode on AND carries hand-drawn shadow sprite art, ArtNem_SStageShadow and Draw_SSShadows, in the same scene. The two are not alternatives. Sonic 2's Special Stage, two paragraphs up, is a different game's scene and a separate enable.

The second correction is the one that sends readers to section 6. The water effects in the Sonic games are not shadow and highlight. They are mid-frame rewrites of CRAM, which is a completely different mechanism with completely different costs, and it is what the next section is about.

Finally, the honest note both of the modern sources volunteer, and it is a fair summary of everything above: not many games used this mode, and the likely reason is the tangle with display priority. Three mechanisms, a precondition about what is on top, two colour indices you have to give up, and a quirk about index 14 is a lot of rules to hold for an effect that a good artist can paint instead.

6 · Mid-frame writes and the CRAM dots

The four-palette limit is not a limit per frame. It is a limit on what the chip is holding at the moment a given scanline is drawn. Rewrite CRAM partway down the frame and the same tiles, drawn from the same art, come out in different colours above and below the split. Games spent that fact heavily, and the instance most players of these games have seen without knowing what they were looking at is the water line.

Sonic 2 does it from an HBlank interrupt armed for the scanline where the water surface currently is. When the interrupt fires, the handler replaces the entire palette:

PalToCRAM:
	move	#$2700,sr
	move.w	#0,(Hint_flag).w
	movem.l	a0-a1,-(sp)
	lea	(VDP_data_port).l,a1
	lea	(Underwater_palette).w,a0 ; load palette from RAM
	move.l	#vdpComm($0000,CRAM,WRITE),VDP_control_port-VDP_data_port(a1)	; set VDP to write to CRAM address $00
    rept 32
	move.l	(a0)+,(a1)	; move palette to CRAM (all 64 colors at once)
    endm
s2disasm/s2.asm:1243-1252 (PalToCRAM, the water surface in Chemical Plant Act 2, Aquatic Ruin and Hidden Palace)

All 64 entries, written as 32 unrolled longword writes with no loop, because a handler running inside the gap at the end of a scanline has very few cycles to spend and a loop's counter is a cost it will not pay. The other half of this chain, arming the interrupt for the right scanline and recomputing that scanline as the camera moves, belongs to the frame and interrupt material rather than here; what belongs here is what those 32 writes cost on screen.

The dots

They cost dots. A write to CRAM while the display is actively drawing puts a pixel of the written colour on the screen at the point the beam had reached. It is a property of the hardware rather than a mistake anyone made, and three independent sources describe it. The rule of thumb is a dosage rule: the more colours you write at once, the more dots appear.

The complementary half of the rule, and the reason the technique is usable at all: writes made while the display is BLANKED produce no dots. Kabuto's hardware notes give the mechanism, that all blanking happens after the palette lookup, so there is nothing on screen for a stray value to land in. This is the whole of the practical advice. Bulk palette work goes in the blanking interval, either through the ports or by DMA; a write during active display is a deliberate effect that has to budget for what it will leave behind.

New here? · How does a game show more than four palettes at once?

It does not, quite. It shows four palettes at a time, twice, in one frame.

The screen is drawn one horizontal line at a time from the top down, and the chip reads CRAM as it goes. So the colours in CRAM when line 40 is drawn are the colours line 40 gets, and if the program changes them before line 120 is drawn, line 120 gets different ones. Nothing about the picture below the change is redrawn or re-uploaded. The same tiles simply read a different set of colours.

That is how the water in a Sonic level works. Above the surface the palette is the ordinary one; the moment the beam crosses the water line the game swaps in a bluer palette, and everything drawn below that line, including Sonic himself, comes out underwater-coloured. It is one of the cheapest big effects on the machine, and it explains something you may have noticed about it: a water line made this way is horizontal by construction. The swap happens between one scanline and the next, so the seam it produces cannot be anything but a straight line across the picture.

Two shipped answers to the same hazard

Sonic 2's answer above is to accept the dots: blast all 64 entries and let them fall where they fall. Sonic 3 and Knuckles takes the other road, and its disassembly says so out loud, which is an unusual thing to find in either file: a comment naming the hardware artefact and the reason for the code's shape. The comment is the disassembly team's, written while working the routine out, not text Sega shipped, and it is quoted here as their reading rather than as the authors'.

; These are used by HInt3 to load the water palette only a small bit at a time,
; this is to space out the CRAM writes to push the VDP dots offscreen.
;
; Each value is an offset into Water_palette.
; From there, 6 bytes, meaning 3 palette entries, are transferred to CRAM.
skdisasm/sonic3k.asm:9308-9312 (the water palette transition tables)

And the handler does exactly that. Three colours, then a deliberately wasted stretch of cycles, then three more:

$$transferColors:
		move.w	(a2)+,d0
		lea	(Water_palette).w,a0
		adda.w	d0,a0
		addi.w	#$C000,d0
		swap	d0
		move.l	d0,VDP_control_port-VDP_data_port(a1)	; write to CRAM at appropriate address
		move.l	(a0)+,(a1)	; transfer two colors
		move.w	(a0)+,(a1)	; transfer the third color
		nop
		nop
		moveq	#$24,d0
		dbf	d0,*	; waste some cycles
		dbf	d1,$$transferColors	; repeat for number of colors
skdisasm/sonic3k.asm:1027-1040 (HInt3, the uneven water surface handler)

The dbf d0,* is a loop that branches to itself and does nothing else, thirty-seven times, purely to burn time: $24 is 36, and dbf runs once more past zero before it falls through. Spacing the bursts out moves each one to a different point in the beam's travel, and the aim, in the comment's own words, is to push the resulting dots off the visible screen. Two shipped games, one hardware artefact, and two entirely different engineering answers to it sitting a disassembly apart.

Gotcha A clean emulator screenshot is not evidence that a game produced no dots. Most emulators do not reproduce CRAM dots at all, and Genesis Plus GX has an open request to add them. Kabuto's notes add the other half: a dot landing near the edge of the picture is mostly invisible on a CRT of the period and plainly visible on modern capture equipment. So how bad this artefact looks has always depended on the display chain, and the tooling most people examine these games with under-reports it.

Games that spent CRAM writes mid-frame

Burning Force: palettes swapped several times per frame, dots left plainly visible Gaiares: a rising water line, with flashing wave sprites over the seam Sonic the Hedgehog: the same water line and the same concealment, six months later identified by rasterscroll from footage with the layers separated

The wave sprites in the last two are the third answer to the hazard, after accepting the dots and spacing them offscreen: draw something over the seam. All three answers are still visible in shipped games, which is a fair picture of how the Genesis was actually programmed. The hardware had an artefact nobody could switch off, and each studio settled for itself whether to hide it, dodge it, or let it show.
