Instructions: Bits, Flow & Blocks

The other half of the Z80 instruction set: the operations that turn bits inside a byte, the jumps and calls that decide what runs next, and the block instructions that copy a whole run of memory in one stroke. The one true prerequisite is the Z80 Programming Model (registers, the pairs, (HL), the flags, dest-first order); no 68000 knowledge is assumed. Every example is real, pulled from the Sonic 2 sound driver and Aeon's own driver and re-verified line by line, unless it is labelled "Teaching example" where it stands. A handful are, and always for the same reason: no local driver uses that instruction, and the box says which and why.

1 · Turning bits

A rotate slides every bit of a byte one place left or right and feeds the bit that falls off one end back in at the other, so nothing is lost, just circulated. The Z80 has two whole families of them, and the split between the families is the story of this section: four cheap one-byte rotates that only ever touch the accumulator A, and a larger, slower set that can rotate or shift any register or the byte in memory at (HL).

One note about the numbers on this page before the entries begin, because a reader arriving from the 68000 track will look for something that is not here. On the Z80 there is no separate add-on cost for naming a memory operand: each form of each instruction has one whole-instruction T-state figure, and that is the number. A rlc (hl) is not "the base cost plus an (hl) surcharge"; it is 15 T-states, full stop. The indexed forms cost more, of course, because the DD/FD prefix and the displacement byte are extra bytes the CPU has to fetch, which is why rlc (ix+d) is 23. That is still one flat number per form, not a mode price you add on yourself. (The Loads & Arithmetic page states this once for the whole track, along with the caveat that T-states are the chip's cost and not the console's wall clock.) The cost chips below show the cheapest form and say so; the collapsed reference box carries the rest.

New here? · What does it mean to "rotate" a byte?

Write a byte as its eight bits in a row, say 1011 0100. A rotate left picks the whole row up and shifts it one place toward the left. The bit that was on the far left has nowhere to go, so it wraps around and reappears on the far right. Do that once and 1011 0100 becomes 0110 1001: every bit moved left one seat, and the leading 1 came back in at the end. Rotate right is the same in the other direction.

Why bother? Because a byte often packs several small fields into its eight bits, and rotating slides the field you care about into a position where you can read or mask it. The driver examples below do exactly that: they rotate a packed sample byte until the nibble they want lines up with the bottom four bits, then mask the rest away.

These are the four one-byte rotates, hard-wired to A. RLCA rotates A left, wrapping the top bit around into the bottom (circular). RRCA does the same to the right. RLA and RRA rotate through carry instead, which the explainer below untangles. Reach for them when you need to move bits inside A cheaply and you do not care about the sign or zero flags, which is most nibble-shuffling work in a sound driver.

The one flag oddity to remember is stated in the row above: these rotates leave S, Z, and P/V untouched. They report only the bit that fell out (in C) and clear H and N. That is the whole reason the second, slower family in the next entry exists: those forms do set S, Z, and parity, so you can branch on the result of the rotate itself.

The driver decodes a packed sample byte by rotating its high nibble down into reach, then masking:

        ld      a,(hl)                  ; 7+3   ; Get next DAC byte
        rlca                            ; 4
        rlca                            ; 4
        rlca                            ; 4
        rlca                            ; 4
        and     0Fh                     ; 7     ; UPPER 4-bit offset into zDACDecodeTbl
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:688-693 (zWriteToDAC, decoding a packed DPCM byte)

Four rlcas rotate the byte left four places, which lifts its high nibble around into the low four bits; and $0F then clears everything above them, so A is left holding a clean 0-to-15 table index. The rotate positions the field and the mask isolates it, the standard nibble-extract pair. The comments are the driver's own cycle counts.

New here? · Rotate with carry vs rotate through carry

This is the split that trips people, and it is worth getting straight once. RLCA is rotate left circular: the eight bits of A form a closed ring, the top bit wraps straight into the bottom, and a copy of that wrapped bit is also dropped into the carry flag C so you can see what came off. The carry is a bystander here; it receives a copy but is not part of the circle.

RLA is rotate left through carry: now C is spliced into the ring as a ninth bit. The top bit of A moves into C, and the old value of C moves into the bottom of A. So the bit that enters at the bottom is whatever was sitting in carry, not the bit that just left the top. This is what lets you shift a value wider than eight bits: the carry carries a bit from one byte into the next.

The 68000 draws the exact same distinction between rol (with carry) and roxl (through the X bit); if you did that track, this is the same idea with different names. See the 68000 rotate page.

RLC / RL / RRC / RR / SLA / SRA / SRL the CB-prefixed rotate and shift group rlc r · rl (hl) · srl (ix+d)

These reach any 8-bit register, (HL), or an indexed byte. They do not reach (BC)/(DE) or a direct address, and you cannot rotate an immediate (rlc 5 is meaningless: there is nothing to write back to).

"CB-prefixed" is a fact about the encoding, and it is where the cost comes from: every instruction in this group is a two-byte opcode, a CB marker byte followed by a byte naming the operation and which register or memory byte it works on. That second fetch is why these cost 8 T-states on a register where the one-byte accumulator rotates cost 4. This family reaches everywhere the accumulator rotates cannot, and sets the flags the accumulator rotates leave alone. The four rotates RLC / RL / RRC / RR mirror RLCA / RLA / RRCA / RRA (circular or through-carry, left or right) but on any register. The three shifts are new: SLA shifts left, filling the bottom with 0; SRL shifts right, filling the top with 0; and SRA shifts right but keeps the top bit as it was.

That SRA-versus-SRL difference is a real fork. SRL is an unsigned halve: it drops a 0 into the top, so $80 becomes $40. SRA is a signed halve: it copies the sign bit back down, so a byte read as a negative number stays negative when you halve it. Pick SRL when the byte is an unsigned count and SRA when it is a signed quantity.

The driver uses srl as the high half of a two-register right shift, threading the bit that falls out through carry into the next byte:

        srl     h                       ; (h >> 1); lowest bit into carry
        rra                             ; (a >> 1); carry from 'h' applied at end
        srl     h                       ; ... and so on ...
        rra
        rra
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:1243-1247 (shifting a 16-bit value held in H:A right)

The value lives across two registers, H on top and A below. srl h shifts the high byte right and drops its lowest bit into carry; rra then rotates that carry into the top of A. So the bit that leaves the bottom of H re-enters the top of A, and the two instructions together shift the whole 16-bit value right by one. This is the everyday way to shift something wider than a single register, and it is exactly the "through carry" rotate earning its keep.

2 · Single bits: BIT, SET, RES

A sound driver keeps a lot of its state as individual flag bits packed into a byte: is this channel playing, is it at rest, should the next note attack. Three instructions poke exactly one bit of a byte each. BIT tests a bit without changing it, SET turns a bit on, and RES turns a bit off. All three take a bit number 0 to 7 and a target that can be any register or a byte in memory.

New here? · Why poke one bit instead of comparing a whole byte?

A byte holds eight independent yes/no facts if you use it that way, one per bit. The driver's track control byte does exactly this: bit 7 is "is this track playing", bit 4 is "do not attack the next note", bit 1 is "the track is at rest", and so on. Packing them into one byte saves seven bytes of RAM per track and lets the driver read or change any one of them without disturbing the others.

You could test bit 7 by AND-ing the byte with $80 and branching on the result, and plenty of Z80 code does. BIT 7,r is the purpose-built version: it does that test in one instruction, sets the zero flag from it, and leaves the byte and the accumulator untouched. SET and RES are its write counterparts, flipping one chosen bit on or off and leaving the rest of the byte alone.

target s A r (B C D E H L) n (HL) (IX+d)/(IY+d) (BC)/(DE) (nn)

The bit number b is 0 to 7. There is no BIT b,n (you cannot test a bit of an immediate) and no BIT b,(BC).

BIT tests one bit and reports it in the zero flag, and the rule for that report is the one thing on this page most worth memorising: BIT sets Z to the COMPLEMENT of the tested bit. If the bit was 0, Z becomes 1; if the bit was 1, Z becomes 0. So bit 7,r followed by jr z jumps exactly when bit 7 was clear, and jr nz jumps when it was set. The complement reads backwards at first, but it is consistent: Z always means "the thing I tested came out zero", and here the thing is a single bit. Reach for BIT whenever a chunk of driver state is one flag bit and you want to branch on it. It touches no register at all.

Two flags in the row above carry a warning. S and P/V are documented UNKNOWN after BIT: the Zilog manual does not define what they hold, so no correct code branches on the sign or parity flag after a BIT. Only Z carries the answer. (H is always set and N always cleared, but nothing useful reads them here.)

The driver checks whether a track is playing before spending time updating it:

        bit     7,(ix+zTrack.PlaybackControl)   ; Is it playing?
        call    nz,zFMUpdateTrack               ; If it is, go update it
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:472-473 (zUpdateFMTracks, per-track update guard)

Bit 7 of each track's control byte is the "is playing" flag; IX points at the track, and (ix+zTrack.PlaybackControl) is that byte. bit 7,... sets Z from bit 7 (Z becomes 0 when the bit is set), and call nz is the reaction: it calls the update routine only when the bit was set, that is, only for a live track. The test and the branch are the two-beat rhythm the whole driver runs on, and here the consumer is right on the next line.

Gotcha The complement is the trap. bit 7,r then jr z,Playing jumps when bit 7 is clear, which is the opposite of what "z, playing" reads like in English. Say it as "Z means the tested bit was zero" and the direction stops flipping on you. It is why the excerpt above uses nz: the track is playing when the bit is set, and set means not-zero.

SET turns the chosen bit to 1 and RES turns it to 0, leaving the other seven bits and, unlike almost everything else, every flag exactly as they were. That "no flags" fact is worth stating plainly: after SET or RES you cannot branch on anything about the write, because it recorded nothing. If you need to know the bit's old value, test it with BIT first; if you just want it a certain way, SET or RES and move on. Reach for them to flip one flag bit of driver state on or off.

The driver clears one control bit when a note's duration ends, and sets another when a track goes quiet:

        res     4,(ix+zTrack.PlaybackControl)   ; When duration over, clear "do not attack" bit 4 (0x10) of track's play control

Each writes one named bit of the same control byte without touching the rest. Aeon's driver does the identical thing with named constants instead of magic bit numbers, which reads better but assembles to the same instruction:

        set     SCF_KEYED_B, (ix+sc_flags)
        res     SCF_KEYED_B, (ix+sc_flags)
aeon/engine/sound/sound_psg.emp:322,362 (keying a channel on, then off; our .emp dialect, same Z80 instructions)

SCF_KEYED_B is a named bit position, so set SCF_KEYED_B,... is set 5,... or whatever the constant resolves to at build time. The instruction underneath is the same SET b,(IX+d); the name is for the human reading the code.

The reader of every one of those writes is the entry above. bit 7,(ix+zTrack.PlaybackControl) tests the same control byte these lines are writing, and call nz spends time on the track only when the answer comes back set: the write and the test are two ends of one convention. Nothing in the byte itself says that bit 1 means "at rest" or that bit 7 means "playing". The bit numbers mean that because the code that sets them and the code that tests them agree, which is why both sides name them with the same constant.

3 · Jumps: JP and JR

A jump changes which instruction runs next by writing a new address into the program counter. The Z80 has two, and choosing between them is a real decision every loop makes. JP jumps to an absolute address you name; JR jumps a short signed distance relative to itself. Both come in an unconditional form and a form that only jumps when a flag condition holds.

New here? · What is a "condition", and where do the flags come from?

A conditional jump does not look at a value; it looks at a flag. The instruction just before it did some arithmetic or logic and left notes in the flag register: was the result zero (the Z flag), did it carry or borrow (the C flag), and so on. jp z,label means "jump only if the Z flag is set"; jr nc,label means "jump only if the carry flag is clear".

So the pattern is always two beats: an instruction that sets flags, then a jump that reads one. The star of that first beat is CP, compare, which subtracts one value from another purely to set the flags and throws the difference away. CP lives on the Loads & Arithmetic page; every example here that starts with a cp is setting up the flag the jump then reads.

JP Jump jp nn · jp cc, nn · jp (hl)

10 T-states taken OR not taken; 3 bytes. JP (HL) is 4 T / 1 byte The key figure: JP cc,nn costs 10 T-states whether or not the branch is taken, unlike JR below. JP (HL) is 4 T and 1 byte; JP (IX)/(IY) are 8 T / 2 bytes. From UM0080, clrhome, and the sigil cost model.
No operand grid: JP's target is a 16-bit address nn, or the register-indirect form (HL) / (IX) / (IY), not a data operand. Its conditions cc are all eight: NZ Z NC C PO PE P M.

JP jumps to an absolute 16-bit address. Reach for it as the general jump: it can land anywhere in the Z80's 64 KB, its conditional form supports every flag condition, and, importantly for a hot loop, its cost does not change when the branch is or is not taken. That last point is why driver code on a timing-critical path prefers jp cc to jr cc: 10 T-states every time, no surprise.

The register-indirect form JP (HL) is the computed jump, and it hides a real misconception. Despite the parentheses, JP (HL) does not read memory. Everywhere else on the Z80 parentheses mean "the byte at this address", but here they do not: jp (hl) jumps to the address that is in HL, it does not fetch anything from (HL) first. It is the Z80's dispatch primitive, the way to jump to an address you computed at run time.

The driver's modulation routine ends by jumping to an address it has been carrying since it started:

        add     hl,bc                           ; Add modulation value
        ex      de,hl
        jp      (hl)                            ; WILL return to zUpdateTrack
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:1047-1049 (jumping to a computed continuation)

The routine popped its own return address into DE on entry, and add hl,bc finishes a frequency in HL, so it is the ex de,hl that puts the address where a computed jump can reach it (and hands the frequency out in DE on the same instruction). Then jp (hl) transfers control straight there, with no memory read in between. The comment names where it lands. A normal jp zUpdateTrack would carry a fixed address baked in at build time; jp (hl) goes wherever the register points right now, which is what makes jump-table dispatch possible.

Gotcha jp (hl) is the one that will catch you: here the parentheses do NOT mean "read memory". Read it as "jump to the address in HL", not "jump to the byte HL points at". The same holds for jp (ix) and jp (iy). The chip's only other liberty with the notation is the IN/OUT family in section 8, where the parentheses name an I/O port instead of a memory address; everywhere else they mean exactly what you expect.

JR Jump Relative jr e · jr cc, e

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touches no flags; like JP it reads a flag for the conditional form and writes none

No operand grid: JR's target is a signed displacement e, written as a label the assembler turns into a distance. Its conditions are only four (see below), not the eight JP accepts.

JR jumps a short signed distance from itself instead of naming an address. That distance is a single signed byte measured relative to the instruction after the JR, giving a reach of -126 to +129 bytes from the JR opcode. Anything farther needs JP. Reach for JR as the everyday short branch: the bottom of a loop jumping back to its top, a two-line skip, an early exit. It is the compact goto, and most branches in a driver are short enough to use it.

Two facts about JR surprise people, and both are load-bearing enough to state in plain prose.

First, the cost story runs the opposite way to the byte story. JR is smaller than JP: 2 bytes against 3. But when the branch is actually taken, JR costs more T-states, not fewer: 12 for a taken JR against 10 for JP, taken or not. So the trade is honest and specific: JR wins on size, JP wins on speed (and JP wins on reach and on having all eight conditions). Choosing JR to "go faster" is backwards; you choose it to save a byte, or because the target is close and a byte matters more than two T-states.

Second, JR takes only four conditions. The only conditional relative jumps are JR NZ, JR Z, JR NC, and JR C, testing the zero and carry flags. There is no JR PO, JR PE, JR P, or JR M: the parity and sign conditions are absolute-jump-only. A newcomer will write jr pe,label expecting it to work; it does not assemble. When you need to branch on parity or sign a short distance away, you use JP, or you restructure so the test is on Z or C.

Here is a cp setting the zero flag and a jr nz reading it, a channel-count check in the driver:

        cp      7                       ; Does it equal 7?  (6 FM channels)
        jr      nz,.silencefm6          ; If not, skip this next part
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:1890-1891 (silencing FM channel 6 when it is not in use)

A song header's channel count of 7 means all six FM channels plus DAC are in use. cp 7 subtracts 7 from A just to set flags (Z becomes 1 only if A was exactly 7), and jr nz jumps past the FM6-silencing code when they are not equal. NZ is one of JR's four legal conditions, so this assembles; had the driver needed to branch on sign here, it would have had to reach for JP. Aeon's driver runs the same shape with jp z instead, since it keeps that path off the hot loop:

        ld      a, (SND_DAC_PHASE)
        cp      2
        jp      z, .draining
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:379-381 (branch to the drain path; our .emp dialect)
Gotcha jr pe,..., jr po,..., jr p,... and jr m,... do not exist. JR only tests Z and C (as NZ, Z, NC, C). Parity and sign branches must use jp cc. And remember the cost: a taken JR is 12 T-states, two more than JP's 10, so JR is the smaller instruction, not the faster one.
Cheaper cousin On a path where the byte count does not matter but the cycle count does, prefer jp cc,nn to jr cc,e: JP is a fixed 10 T-states taken or not, while a taken JR is 12. On a path where ROM size matters and the target is within reach, JR saves a byte per branch. The driver deliberately keeps its hottest loops on jp for exactly the 10-versus-12 reason.

4 · DJNZ, the signature loop

One instruction gets its own section because it is the Z80's most characteristic loop and because its counter behaves in a way worth slowing down for. DJNZ means Decrement B and Jump if Not Zero: in a single instruction it subtracts one from register B and, if the result is not zero, jumps a short relative distance. Put a count in B, write the loop body, end with djnz label, and you have a counted loop.

DJNZ Decrement B and Jump if Not Zero djnz e

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touches NO flags, even though it decrements B; you cannot test a flag about B after DJNZ
13 T taken8 T not taken 2 bytes; same relative reach as JR 13 T-states when it loops, 8 when B reaches zero and it falls through. 2 bytes. It is cheaper than the two-instruction dec b + jr nz (which would be 16/11 T and would clobber the flags). From UM0080, clrhome, and the sigil cost model.
counter A B C D E H L BC DE HL

That row is a one-off, not the standard 8-bit row every other grid on these pages uses: DJNZ has no addressing forms to offer, only a choice of counter register, so the chips are registers. The nine crosses are the point: DJNZ counts in B and nothing else. Not C, not the pair BC, not any other register. If your count needs to live somewhere else, DJNZ cannot loop on it.

DJNZ hard-wires B as its counter, jumps by the same short signed displacement as JR (-126 to +129 bytes), and, despite decrementing a register, changes no flags at all. That last fact is useful: a DJNZ loop can carry a flag set inside the loop body all the way out to a test after the loop, because the decrement never disturbs it. Reach for DJNZ whenever you have a count that fits in a byte and want the tightest possible counted loop.

The exit rule is the thing to hold onto: DJNZ exits when B reaches zero, so loading B with N runs the body exactly N times. No off-by-one. Load 3, the body runs three times. (The one edge: loading B with 0 does not skip the loop; the first decrement wraps 0 to 255, so it runs 256 times. Guard a possibly-zero count before the loop.)

The driver's per-channel update loop is the canonical shape, counter in B, body in the middle, djnz at the bottom:

.fmloop:
        push    bc
        ld      de,zTrack.len                   ; Spacing between tracks
        add     ix,de                           ; Next track
        bit     7,(ix+zTrack.PlaybackControl)   ; Is it playing?
        call    nz,zFMUpdateTrack               ; If it is, go update it
        pop     bc
        djnz    .fmloop
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:468-475 (zUpdateFMTracks, one pass per FM channel)

B holds the channel count. Each pass advances IX to the next track, tests and maybe updates it, and djnz decrements B and loops until it hits zero. Notice the push bc / pop bc bracketing the body: the update call clobbers BC, so the loop saves and restores the counter around it. That is the everyday reason to push a register, and it is the counter-register discipline the aside below warns about, in the flesh.

DJNZ also loops on itself with no body at all, as a calibrated delay:

        djnz    $                       ; 8     ; Busy wait for specific amount of time in 'b'
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:683 (and again at :705; timing the DAC sample rate)

The $ means "here", so djnz $ jumps back to itself and spins B times, burning a precise amount of time set by whatever is in B. The driver uses it to pace DAC sample playback: a larger B waits longer between samples, which lowers the playback rate. A whole loop in one two-byte instruction.

New here? · How DJNZ compares to the 68000's dbf

If you did the 68000 track, DJNZ is its dbf, with one difference that will bite if you carry the habit across. Both are one-instruction counted loops that keep the count in a register the body must not casually reuse. But they exit on different values.

dbf exits when the decrement produces -1, so it runs the body one more time than the number you load: load 3, the body runs four times, and that is why Sonic 2 writes -1 into every table-length loop count. DJNZ exits when B reaches 0, so it runs the body exactly the number you load: load 3, the body runs three times. Same idea, off by one from each other. The 68000 flow page has the dbf side; the toy below is deliberately the same shape as that page's counter so you can feel the difference. And in both machines the counter register is precious: reuse it inside the body without saving it and the loop count is corrupted, which is exactly why the driver loop above brackets its body with push bc / pop bc.

5 · Calls, returns, and RST

A call is a jump that leaves a note so control can come back. CALL pushes the address of the instruction after itself onto the stack, then jumps; RET pops that address back and jumps to it. Both have conditional forms. Two more returns, RETI and RETN, exist for interrupt handlers, and RST is a one-byte call to a handful of fixed addresses.

A quick reminder · the stack and the return address

The full story lives on the Programming Model page, section 6, which has a stack that grows downward and a stack pointer SP. Pocket version: the stack is a region of RAM, SP points at its top, PUSH steps down and writes, POP reads and steps up. A return address is just two bytes on that stack; nothing marks them as special. CALL writes them, RET reads them back, and the pairing is a convention the code keeps with itself. This section does not re-teach push and pop; it uses them.

CALL Call subroutine call nn · call cc, nn

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touches no flags, so a routine can hand its result back to the caller in the flags
17 T taken10 T not taken 3 bytes; unconditional CALL nn is always 17 T CALL cc,nn costs 17 T-states when it calls and 10 when the condition fails. CALL nn is always 17. Both are 3 bytes. Conditions cc are all eight. From UM0080, clrhome, and the sigil cost model.
No operand grid: CALL takes a 16-bit address nn. There is no register-indirect CALL (no call (hl)); computed calls are done by arranging the address and using jp (hl) into a routine that returns for you, or by pushing a return address by hand.

CALL is how one routine invokes another and gets back. Reach for its conditional form to invoke a routine only when a flag holds, which the driver does constantly to skip work. Here it plays a new sound only when the queued byte is not the "nothing queued" sentinel:

        ld      a,(zAbsVar.QueueToPlay)
        cp      80h
        call    nz,zPlaySoundByIndex            ; If not 80h, we need to play something new!
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:427-429 (playing a queued sound if one is pending)

$80 is the "nothing queued" marker. cp 80h sets Z from the comparison, and call nz is the reaction: it calls the play routine only when the queued byte differs from $80. The compare sets the flag and the conditional call consumes it, with no separate branch instruction needed. A plain call nz is often cheaper than branching around an unconditional call.

RET Return from subroutine ret · ret cc

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touches no flags; the flags the routine set survive the return for the caller to read
10 T plain RET11 T taken5 T not taken 1 byte; the second and third are the conditional RET cc RET cc is 11 T-states when it returns and just 5 when it does not, the cheapest conditional in the whole instruction set. Plain RET is 10 T. Both 1 byte. From UM0080, clrhome, and the sigil cost model.
No operand grid: RET takes no operand. Its one implicit source is the top of the stack. Conditions cc are all eight.

RET pops the return address off the stack into PC, so execution resumes in the caller. Its conditional form is a small idiom worth knowing: ret z or ret nz returns early when a flag holds and otherwise falls through to the rest of the routine, and at 5 T-states for the not-taken case it is the cheapest conditional structure available, which is why guard-style ret cc is everywhere in driver code. Because RET touches no flags, a routine can set a flag on its last real instruction and let the caller branch on it after the return; the answer travels back in the flags.

        ret
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:367 (the end of zWriteFMI)

A plain ret ends the routine by popping the address the matching call pushed. Nothing more; the whole return mechanism is that one pop.

RETI / RETN Return from Interrupt / from Non-maskable interrupt reti · retn

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touch no flags
14 T-states 2 bytes each Both are 2 bytes and 14 T-states. From UM0080, clrhome, and the sigil cost model.
No operand grid: neither takes an operand.

These are the interrupt-handler returns, and on the Genesis they are mostly a story about why you do NOT need them. RETI returns like RET but also pulses an interrupt-acknowledge sequence so that daisy-chained Z80-family peripherals know the handler has finished. The Genesis has no such peripherals watching the Z80 bus, so nothing is listening for that pulse. RETN returns from a non-maskable interrupt and restores the interrupt-enable state that the NMI masked, which normal Genesis sound code does not exercise either.

What real driver code does instead is return from its one interrupt with a plain ei then ret. The Genesis Z80 takes its single interrupt through RST 38h (interrupt mode 1, section 6), and a bare ret is all it takes to leave the handler:

        ei
        ret
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:535-536 (the VBlank handler returning; our .emp dialect)
Teaching example · not engine code 
        // a handler that ended with RETI would look like:
        ei
        reti                            // return AND acknowledge to a daisy-chained device

No driver here does this, because there is no daisy-chained device to acknowledge. The census found zero reti instructions across the Sonic 2 driver, the S3K driver, and Aeon's driver: they all return with ei / ret as shown above. RETI is in the instruction set, but on this machine it has no job the plain return does not already do.

RST Restart rst p

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touches no flags
11 T-states 1 byte One byte, 11 T-states (5, 3, 3). From UM0080 and clrhome.
No operand grid: p must be one of eight fixed page-zero addresses, 00h 08h 10h 18h 20h 28h 30h 38h. Anything else (RST 12h) does not exist.

RST is a one-byte CALL to one of eight fixed low addresses. It pushes the return address exactly like CALL and pairs with RET, but because the target is one of only eight slots, the whole instruction fits in a single byte instead of three. Reach for it to call a hot routine you use constantly, where saving two bytes per call across the whole driver adds up, and where you can afford to place that routine at one of the eight aligned addresses. The driver does this for its FM-chip wait routine:

        rst     zFMBusyWait ; 'rst' is like 'call' but only works for 8-byte aligned addresses <= 38h
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:356 (a one-byte call to the FM-ready wait; the comment is the driver's own)

zFMBusyWait sits at one of the eight aligned slots, so the driver reaches it with a one-byte rst rather than a three-byte call. The source comment even states the constraint. The busiest of the eight slots is 38h, which does double duty: it is also the address the hardware jumps to on an interrupt in mode 1, so the VBlank handler is assembled right at it:

        org     38h
zVInt:    rsttarget
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:392-393 (placing the VBlank handler at the IM 1 vector)

org 38h tells the assembler to put the handler at address $0038, so the hardware's implicit jump-to-$38 on an interrupt lands exactly on zVInt. Section 6 leans on this: on the Genesis, RST 38h and the interrupt vector are the same address.

6 · Waiting and interrupts

Five short instructions manage doing nothing and handling interrupts. NOP does nothing for four T-states. DI and EI turn maskable interrupts off and on. IM picks how the CPU responds to one. HALT stops the CPU until an interrupt wakes it. Two of these carry a real footgun each, and both footguns are stated below in plain prose because a reader who skips the asides still has to know them.

DI / EI Disable Interrupts / Enable Interrupts di · ei

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touch no flags
4 T-states 1 byte each Both are one byte and 4 T-states. From UM0080 and clrhome.
No operands.

DI disables maskable interrupts and EI re-enables them, and drivers bracket a timing-critical stretch between them so nothing interrupts it. But EI has one behaviour that is load-bearing and catches people: EI does not take effect immediately. Interrupts stay disabled until after the instruction following EI has executed. This is deliberate, and it is why the canonical end of an interrupt handler is ei then ret: the one-instruction delay lets the ret finish and pop the return address before any new interrupt can fire, so interrupts cannot pile up on the stack. (DI, by contrast, takes effect at once.)

The driver brackets its cycle-sensitive DAC write with the two:

        di                              ; 4     ; disable interrupts (while updating DAC)
        ei                              ; 4     ; enable interrupts (done updating DAC, busy waiting for next update)
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:685,703 (guarding the timing-critical DAC output)

DAC output is sensitive to exact timing, so the inner write runs with interrupts off and re-enables them during the wait for the next sample. The di takes hold immediately; the ei lets one more instruction run before interrupts can actually arrive.

New here? · Why would enabling interrupts wait one instruction?

Picture the end of an interrupt handler. The handler is about to run ei then ret, and the ret is what pops the return address off the stack and jumps back to the interrupted code. If ei took effect the instant it ran, a second interrupt could fire in the tiny gap before the ret, pushing another return address and re-entering the handler. Do that fast enough and the stack fills with return addresses until it overflows.

Zilog's fix is to delay the enable by exactly one instruction, so the instruction right after ei is guaranteed to run first. Put the ret there and it always completes before the next interrupt is allowed in. The handler leaves cleanly, one return address at a time.

IM Interrupt Mode im 0 · im 1 · im 2

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touch no flags
8 T-states 2 bytes each Each of IM 0 / IM 1 / IM 2 is 2 bytes and 8 T-states. From UM0080 and clrhome.
No operand grid: the operand is the mode number, 0, 1, or 2.

IM selects how the Z80 responds to a maskable interrupt mode. In IM 1, any interrupt jumps to the fixed address $0038. In IM 2, the CPU builds the handler address from the I register and a byte the device supplies, indexing a table of handler pointers. IM 0 reads an instruction off the bus from the interrupting device. Genesis sound code universally uses IM 1: the vertical-blank interrupt line vectors through $0038, which (from section 5) is also where RST 38h goes, so the handler sits at that one address. Why the Genesis Z80 is wired this way is a hardware story that belongs to the hardware track, not here.

The driver's opening breath sets it all up: interrupts off, mode 1, stack pointed at its top.

        di
        im      1                       // VBlank /INT -> RST 38h -> $0038
        ld      sp, SND_STACK_TOP       // stack top $1FFE (z80-ram-map spec)
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:163-165 (driver init; our .emp dialect)

di masks interrupts while the driver sets itself up, im 1 selects the mode whose vector is $0038, and ld sp,... plants the stack. The comment traces the interrupt path the mode chooses: the VBlank /INT line becomes an implicit RST 38h, which lands at $0038.

HALT / NOP Halt / No Operation halt · nop

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hunaff. P/Vunaff. Nunaff. Cunaff. touch no flags
4 T-states 1 byte each; HALT's 4 T repeats until an interrupt arrives NOP is 1 byte / 4 T. HALT is 1 byte, and its 4 T-states are per internal cycle: it keeps executing an internal no-op (to keep memory refresh alive) and repeats until an interrupt or reset wakes it. From UM0080 and clrhome.
No operands.

NOP does nothing and costs four T-states, used for fine padding or timing. HALT suspends the CPU until an interrupt arrives, then resumes at the instruction after it. It is the polite way to idle: instead of a busy-wait loop, the CPU stops and waits for the next interrupt to wake it.

But HALT carries the section's second footgun, and it must be on the spine: on the Genesis, di then halt is a lockup. The Z80 leaves HALT for one of three things: an interrupt it is allowed to accept, a non-maskable interrupt on the /NMI pin, or a reset. DI shuts the first one out. And the second one is not available here, because the Mega Drive never asserts the Z80's /NMI line and no register on the machine lets a program pull it. So the only way out is the third: the 68000 writing the Z80 reset register at $A11200, which restarts the sound CPU from address zero with its flags, interrupt mode and stack all gone, and takes the YM2612's reset line with it, because the two are wired together. That is a crash recovery, not a wake-up. Never halt with interrupts masked.

Teaching example · not engine code 
        // idle until the next VBlank interrupt (interrupts ENABLED):
        ei
        halt                            // CPU sleeps here; the interrupt wakes it

        // the lockup: NEVER do this
        di
        halt                            // waits forever; nothing can wake it but a reset

Genesis sound drivers rarely halt at all: the Sonic 2 and S3K drivers are paced by the 68000's VBlank interrupt and busy-wait with djnz $ instead, and Aeon's driver runs a di-bracketed streaming loop. The census found no trustworthy real halt in any of them, so this is a labelled teaching example rather than a driver excerpt.

7 · Block instructions

The Z80's block instructions fold a whole memory loop into a single opcode. One of them copies a byte from (HL) to (DE), steps both pointers, and counts BC down, all at once; the repeating variant re-runs itself until the count hits zero, so a whole block copy is one instruction. There are eight, in two groups of four: the block copy group copies bytes, the block compare group searches them.

LDI / LDIR / LDD / LDDR Load (and Increment/Decrement) (and Repeat) ldi · ldir · ldd · lddr

Flags Sunaff. Zunaff. Hclear P/Vcount Nclear Cunaff. S, Z and C are left alone; H and N are cleared; P/V is the counter (set while bytes remain, clear when BC hits 0)
16 T single step21 T per repeat 2 bytes; the final pass of a repeat is 16 T LDI/LDD are 2 bytes / 16 T. LDIR/LDDR cost 21 T for every iteration that repeats (while BC is not yet zero) and 16 T for the final one, so copying N bytes costs 21·(N-1) + 16 T-states. From UM0080 and clrhome.
No operand grid: the operands are all implicit. HL is the source pointer, DE the destination pointer, BC the count. You set those three registers first; the instruction takes no written operands.

LDI copies the byte at (HL) to (DE), then increments both pointers and decrements BC. LDD is the same but decrements the pointers (walking backward). LDIR and LDDR are the repeating forms: they do that step over and over until BC reaches zero, so a single ldir copies a whole block. Reach for LDIR as the Z80's memcpy.

The flag behaviour is load-bearing and genuinely odd, so here it is in prose. These do NOT set the zero flag from the copied data; they clear H and N and, most importantly, use P/V as a counter flag: after the step, P/V is set while bytes still remain to copy and cleared once BC has reached zero. (That is why the repeating LDIR, which stops precisely when BC hits zero, leaves P/V clear at the end.) The reason the family exists at all is exactly this: plain DEC BC sets no flags, so you cannot tell when a hand-written copy loop is done without an extra ld a,b / or c test each pass. The block instructions bake the counter status into P/V so the loop needs nothing extra.

One more fact that matters for a real-time driver: the repeating forms are interruptible between repeats. LDIR is not one uninterruptible marathon; the CPU re-executes it each pass, so an interrupt can be recognised in the gap between one copied byte and the next, and the copy resumes where it left off. A long LDIR therefore does not lock out the VBlank interrupt for its whole duration. (And a corner case: if BC is zero when a repeating form begins, it does not copy nothing; the first decrement wraps BC to $FFFF and it copies 65536 bytes.)

New here? · How can one instruction be a whole loop?

An ordinary instruction runs once and the program counter moves on. A repeating block instruction like LDIR does not: after it copies a byte and counts BC down, if BC is still not zero it steps the program counter backward onto itself, so the very next thing the CPU fetches is the same LDIR again. It keeps landing back on itself until BC reaches zero, and only then does the program counter move past it.

That re-landing is also what makes it interruptible. Because each pass is a fresh execution of the instruction, the CPU checks for an interrupt between passes exactly as it would between any two instructions. So an LDIR copying hundreds of bytes is really hundreds of tiny re-executions with interrupt-sized gaps between them, not one long stretch with the door bolted. The toy below re-lands on the instruction once per Step; a real CPU does it a few million times a second.

The driver copies a run of track-init fields with one ldir, the C loop spelled out in its own comment:

        ldir                            ; while (bc-- > 0) *de++ = *hl++; (copy track address, default key offset, default volume)
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:1871 (copying header fields into a track's RAM)

The non-optimized build of the same driver spells the identical copy out as four single ldis, one field per line, which shows the auto-increment plainly:

        ldi                             ; *de++ = *hl++ (copy track address low byte from header to track's copy of this value)
        ldi                             ; *de++ = *hl++ (copy track address high byte from header to track's copy of this value)
        ldi                             ; *de++ = *hl++ (default key offset, typically 0, can be set later by coord flag E9)
        ldi                             ; *de++ = *hl++ (track default volume)
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:1873-1876 (the same copy, unrolled)

Each ldi moves one byte from (HL) to (DE) and bumps both pointers, so four of them copy four consecutive fields, exactly what the single ldir above does when BC is 4.

There is a delightful idiom that turns LDIR into a memory fill. Point DE one byte ahead of HL, seed the first byte, and let ldir smear it forward: each copy reads the byte just written, so one seed value spreads across the whole region.

        ld      hl, SND_SEQ_BASE
        ld      de, SND_SEQ_BASE+1
        ld      bc, SND_SEQ_END-SND_SEQ_BASE-1
        ld      (hl), 0                 // seed the first byte; ldir smears it forward
        ldir
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:1116-1120 (zeroing a RAM region; our .emp dialect)

Because DE = HL + 1, the first ldir step copies the freshly-seeded byte at (HL) into the next byte, then both pointers advance and the next step copies that byte forward, and so on. One written byte becomes a whole region of that value. It is a memset built from a block copy that overlaps itself on purpose.

CPI / CPIR / CPD / CPDR Compare (and Increment/Decrement) (and Repeat) cpi · cpir · cpd · cpdr

Flags Sresult Zresult Hresult P/Vcount Nset Cunaff. Z means A matched the byte, P/V means bytes still remain; N is set, C untouched; reading those two together is the idiom
16 T single step21 T per repeat 2 bytes; the final pass of a repeat is 16 T Same timing as the block copy group: CPI/CPD 2 bytes / 16 T; CPIR/CPDR 21 T per repeating iteration, 16 T for the last. From UM0080 and clrhome.
No operand grid: all implicit. A is the value searched for, HL the pointer walked, BC the count. There is no (DE) here; nothing is written.

The block compare group searches instead of copying. Each step compares A against the byte at (HL) (subtracting to set flags, discarding the result, exactly like CP), then steps HL and decrements BC. The repeating forms stop on either of two conditions: a match (A equals the byte) or the count running out. That is why the flags carry a two-part answer, and it is the idiom worth remembering: after CPIR, read Z to learn whether a match was found and P/V to learn whether the count ran out. One flag says "did I find it", the other says "did I search everything".

Teaching example · not engine code 
        ld      hl, table               // where to search
        ld      bc, table_len           // how many bytes
        ld      a, target               // the byte to find
        cpir                            // scan until A == (HL) or BC == 0
        jr      z, found                // Z set means a match; P/V clear would mean ran out

The census found no cpir or cpi anywhere in the Sonic 2 driver, the S3K driver, or Aeon's driver, so this is a labelled teaching example rather than a real excerpt. The sound drivers simply do not scan byte runs this way; the instruction is shown for completeness and because its Z-plus-P/V split is the same counter idea the block copy group uses.

8 · What the Genesis never uses

The Z80 has a whole family of instructions this page has not mentioned: IN and OUT, plus their block forms, which read and write the Z80's separate 256-port I/O address space over a dedicated line of its own, /IORQ. They are real Z80 instructions. On the Sega Genesis they go essentially unused, and the reason is a board-design choice, not a property of the Z80.

On this console the Z80's sound hardware is memory-mapped: the YM2612 FM chip and the PSG sit at ordinary addresses in the Z80's 64 KB memory space (the YM2612 around $4000, the PSG at $7F11), so the driver reaches them with the same LD instructions it uses for anything else, never with IN or OUT. The /IORQ line simply is not wired to anything the sound program needs. Across the three local sound drivers (the Sonic 2 driver, the S3K driver, and Aeon's driver), no I/O instruction appears in any of them; the whole family is one collapsed reference below, present for completeness but not part of the working vocabulary here. Attribution matters: memory-mapping the sound chips is Sega's board design, not something the Z80 does on its own.

The I/O family, for completeness

The dedicated I/O group, none of which the Genesis sound path uses:

InstructionWhat it does
IN A,(n)Read the byte from I/O port n into A. (n drives A0 to A7; A itself rides the top half of the address bus.) Sets no flags.
IN r,(C)Read into register r from the port the BC pair addresses: C drives A0 to A7 and B drives A8 to A15. Most peripherals decode only the low half, so in practice C picks the port and B rides along. Sets S/Z/H/P-V/N like a load-test.
OUT (n),AWrite A to I/O port n. Sets no flags.
OUT (C),rWrite register r to the port the BC pair addresses, C on A0 to A7 and B on A8 to A15, the same as IN r,(C). Sets no flags.
INI / INIR / IND / INDRBlock input: read one port repeatedly into a run of memory, walking HL and counting down B, with repeating forms.
OUTI / OTIR / OUTD / OTDRBlock output: write a run of memory to a port, the mirror of the input block ops.

These drive the Z80's separate I/O space through its dedicated I/O-request line, /IORQ. Do not confuse it with /BUSRQ, which is the line the 68000 pulls at $A11100 to take the Z80's bus away. On the Genesis /IORQ is not used for sound, so timing and encoding for the group are out of scope here. From UM0080; the absence in local drivers is from the examples census.

There is one genuinely rare register worth a closing anecdote, from the deep-reference corner. The R register (memory refresh, covered on the Programming Model page) counts opcode fetches on its own, so its low bits are always changing. The S3K driver reads it as a cheap ever-changing value:

        ld      a, r                            ; Get memory refresh register
        ld      (unk_1C17), a                   ; Save it
skdisasm/Sound/Z80 Sound Driver.asm:478-479 (reading R as a pseudo-random seed)

ld a,r pulls the refresh count into A, and the driver stashes it as a weak pseudo-random seed. It is not a real random-number generator (the pattern is entirely predictable), but for a shimmer or a bit of variety it is free entropy already sitting in the CPU. A rare, real use of a register most code never touches.

Read the loop you started with. The .fmloop excerpt in the DJNZ entry is one pass of a real sound driver's frame, and it is made of nothing but this page and its sibling. push bc parks the counter where the call cannot reach it, an add walks IX to the next track, bit 7,(ix+zTrack.PlaybackControl) asks one bit of one control byte whether that track is playing, call nz spends time on it only if the answer was yes, pop bc hands the counter back, and djnz closes the loop and decides whether to go round again. The test, the conditional call and the loop are all taught here; the push, the pop and the 16-bit add come from Loads & Arithmetic. One pass per FM channel, and it is the shape most of a driver is written in.

From here, the instruction index reaches every entry on both pages, and The Programming Model is where the registers, the pairs, (HL) and the flags all of these instructions move are taught in full.
