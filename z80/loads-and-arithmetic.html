1 · How to read a Z80 instruction

The Programming Model page taught the machine; this page teaches the verbs. Three of its rules govern every line below, so here they are in one place before the instructions start. Each is taught in full on that page; these are the pocket versions.

A quick reminder · destination first, parentheses mean memory, and where the values live

Destination first. The Z80 writes the receiving side on the left. ld a, b copies B into A; add a, 3 puts A + 3 back into A. Read every line "into the left, from the right." (Coming from the 68000 track, this is the one habit to flip: that assembler writes the source first.)

Parentheses mean memory. A bare name is a register; the same name in parentheses is the byte in memory at the address that register holds. ld a, hl does not exist, but ld a, (hl) reads the byte HL points at. (nn) means the byte at a literal address written into the instruction.

A is where 8-bit arithmetic happens. A is the accumulator: the 8-bit add, subtract, compare, and the logic operations all read one operand from A and write the result back into A. Many single-register operations (INC, DEC, the bit operations) and all the 16-bit ones go around it. The full model, including the register pairs and the flag register, is on The Programming Model.

Every entry below opens the same way: the mnemonic and what it stands for, then a scannable metadata block before any prose. Three parts make up that block, and the first two need a word of explanation now because they are Z80-specific. One convention before them: Z80 assemblers ignore case, so LD A,B and ld a,b assemble to the same byte. This wiki writes the syntax lines and the quoted code lowercase, the way the drivers themselves are written, and names an instruction in prose by its uppercase mnemonic (LD, CP, DJNZ).

The FLAGS row shows the six bits of the flag register F in their own order, S Z H P/V N C, and marks what the instruction does to each: result (set or cleared to match the result), set (always 1), clear (always 0), fixed (a constant, but a different constant per mnemonic in the group, the way N is 0 after ADD and 1 after SUB), unaff. (left exactly as it was), or undef. (the manual documents no value, so the flag moved but what it holds is not yours to read; BIT on the sibling page leaves S and P/V that way). Only unaff. means the instruction left the flag alone, and it is the only one drawn dim. An instruction that moves no flags at all gets an honest caption saying so. Hover or focus any chip for that flag's meaning.

The COST line is measured in T-states, the Z80's own clock ticks. The chip shows the cheapest form and says so, and the line beside it names the other forms; where an instruction has more of them than that line can carry, the full per-form table lives in a collapsed refbox at the end of the entry. Three things to know coming in. First, there is no additive addressing-mode rule on the Z80: a 68000 reader learns to add a per-mode price on top of a base cost, but the Z80's manual just lists a flat T-state count for each whole form, and that is the number the chip shows. The nearest thing to a surcharge is the DD/FD prefix byte that turns an HL form into an IX/IY one, and it is a flat +4 T-states and +1 byte for the prefix itself: ld hl,nn is 10, ld ix,nn is 14. Where the indexed form also carries a displacement byte, that byte has to be fetched too and the gap widens, which is why ld r,(hl) is 7 while ld r,(ix+d) is 19. Even then, the number in the table is the number; there is nothing you add on yourself. Second, T-states are the Z80's ticks, not 68000 cycles; the Z80 runs its own clock (about 3.58 MHz on an NTSC Genesis), so the two counts are not directly comparable and this page never mixes them. They can be related, because every clock in the console is the same crystal divided and the 68000 gets exactly 15 of its cycles for every 7 the Z80 gets (the ratio is on The Machine); convert through that ratio if you need to, but never add the two counts together.

Third, and the one that bites: these are the chip's own costs, not the console's wall clock. A T-state count tells you what the Z80 would take with the bus to itself. On a Genesis it rarely has the bus to itself: the 68000 can take it away entirely ($A11100), and a fetch through the $8000 cartridge window waits on the main bus, which is why real driver code carries hand-written notes like ; 7+3 where the 3 is a wait, not an instruction. Use the T-state numbers to compare two ways of writing the same thing, which is what they are good for. Do not use them to predict how many microseconds a routine takes on real hardware.

The OPERANDS grid shows, for each slot the instruction really has, one chip per addressing form: lit if that form is legal there, crossed out if it is not. A few forms are legal only in combination with what the other slot holds; those chips are drawn dashed and tagged with the register they are tied to, and the entry's note under the grid names them. Two fixed rows are learned once and scanned everywhere. The 8-bit row has seven slots and the 16-bit row has six:

8-bit forms Ar (B C D E H L)n (HL)(IX+d)/(IY+d)(BC)/(DE)(nn)
16-bit forms BC DE HLSPIX IY AFnn(nn)

r is any of the six general 8-bit registers B C D E H L (plus A, shown as its own chip because so many instructions single it out). n is an immediate value, a constant written into the instruction. (nn) is the byte (or word) at a literal 16-bit address. The crosses carry as much information as the lit chips: a row that is mostly crosses tells you at a glance how picky the instruction is, and each entry's note names the tempting lines that will not assemble and the fix.

2 · LD: the one instruction that moves everything

Most processors have a small zoo of move instructions. The Z80 has one, and its name is LD, for Load. Every ordinary copy of a byte or a word between registers and memory is an LD: register to register, a constant into a register, a byte read from memory, a byte written to memory. There is no separate "store" or "move"; the direction is decided entirely by which side you write first. Three things do move data without being an LD, and each is a special case you meet later: PUSH and POP, which are its close cousins over the stack (section 3), EX (SP),HL, which trades a pair with the word on top of it (section 4), and the block copy on the sibling page, which is the machine's only memory-to-memory move. Learn where LD is allowed to reach and you have learned the Z80's whole moving company, which is why the operand grid below carries most of the lesson.

A load's cost is set by its form, not by an added addressing-mode price: ld r,n is 7, ld r,(HL) is 7, ld A,(nn) is 13, ld r,(IX+d) is 19. Storing costs the same as loading in each shape (ld (HL),r is 7, ld (nn),A is 13, ld (IX+d),r is 19); the one that is not a mirror is ld (HL),n at 10.
Destination Ar (B C D E H L)n (HL)(IX+d)/(IY+d)(BC)/(DE)(nn)

The two rows look permissive, but three couplings are where it bites, and the dashed "A only" chips mark two of them. The crossed n on the destination row is the small one: you cannot write into an immediate, because a constant is not a place. The three real refusals: ld (hl),(hl) does not exist (no 8-bit move goes memory to memory; that opcode is HALT), so you route through a register. (BC) and (DE) pair only with A: ld a,(de) and ld (de),a assemble, but ld b,(de) does not. And the direct-address form (nn) is also A-only for a single byte: ld (nn),a works, ld (nn),b does not. Any register can reach memory through (HL) or (IX+d); only A gets the narrow (BC)/(DE)/(nn) shortcuts.

Plainly: LD copies a value from where the right operand says into where the left operand says, and leaves the source exactly as it was. Reach for it constantly, because it is how every value gets anywhere: into A to be worked on, out to memory to be kept, from one register to another to free the first. It dominates real code by a wide margin: the Sonic 2 sound driver this page quotes runs 747 LDs against 119 of the next most common instruction. Two obscure forms are the one exception to the no-flags rule, ld a,i and ld a,r, which read the interrupt-vector and refresh registers and do set S, Z, and P/V; no load in the grid above touches a flag.

New here? · "Load" sounds like it empties the source. It does not.

The word load makes it sound like the value is picked up and carried, leaving an empty spot behind. It is not. LD makes a copy: after ld a, b, the value is in A and still in B, both. Nothing is emptied, nothing is moved in the everyday sense. This is the same lesson the 68000 track teaches for its MOVE instruction (see Moving Data); the mnemonic differs, the idea is identical. If you need the source cleared afterward, that is a second instruction.

Why does this matter? Because a great deal of Z80 code depends on the source surviving. You copy B into A precisely so you can do arithmetic on it in A while B keeps the original for later. A load that destroyed its source would make that impossible.

The toy runs four real loads against a handful of registers and a four-byte patch of memory that HL points into. Watch the destination change, the source stay put, and the parentheses decide whether a register or a memory byte is touched.

Now the same instruction in the sound driver. Inside its DAC playback loop, HL is a pointer into a compressed sample stream, and this pulls the next byte out to decode:

        ld      a,(hl)                  ; 7+3   ; Get next DAC byte
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:688 (SMPS Z80 driver)

That single line reads the byte HL currently points at into A, where the next few instructions will pick it apart. The consumer is immediate: right after this the driver rotates and masks A to extract a nibble, work that only makes sense once the byte is in the accumulator. (The ; 7+3 in the comment is the driver author's own T-state note: 7 for the load plus 3 wait-states for that particular memory, a reminder that timing is what a sound loop lives or dies by.)

The engine's own driver, written in Aeon's .emp dialect, shows the pointer being built a byte at a time. The .emp source uses // for comments instead of ;, but the Z80 instructions are exactly the same. Here H already holds a fixed page and the code sets L, so together HL names one slot of a small ring buffer, then reads it:

        ld      l, c                    // l = RD (h = $19)
        ld      a, (hl)                 // ring[rd]
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:367-368 (Aeon driver, .emp dialect)

The first line sets the low half of the pointer to the read cursor in C; the second dereferences the whole pair. Two loads, and the byte the loop wants is in A. This is the LD mover's ld a, (hl) button, running for real.

Gotcha · the move that is not there

The tempting ld (hl),(hl) and ld bc,de do not exist. No LD moves memory to memory, and none moves a pair to a pair (the only 16-bit register-to-register loads are ld sp,hl, ld sp,ix, and ld sp,iy). The machine does have one memory-to-memory move, and it is not an LD: the block copy group on the sibling page (LDI and its repeating kin) copies a byte from (HL) to (DE), locked to those two pairs and no others. To copy BC into DE you move the halves, ld d,b then ld e,c, or push one pair and pop the other. The machine gives you narrow doors and expects you to route through them.

Cheaper cousins

To put zero in A, prefer xor a (1 byte, 4 T-states) over ld a, 0 (2 bytes, 7 T-states): both leave A = 0, but xor a is smaller and faster (it also sets the flags, which ld never does; see XOR). For any other constant, ld a, n is exactly the tool and has no cheaper cousin.

3 · 16-bit loads and the stack

LD also moves whole 16-bit register pairs, and the stack instructions PUSH and POP are its close cousins: they move a pair to or from a special region of memory. This section covers both, and one honest contrast with the 68000 that a reader from that track will want early.

AF is crossed on both rows on purpose: there is no ld af,nn and no 16-bit load that names AF at all. AF moves as a unit only through push af/pop af and ex af,af', because its low half is the flag register, not data you load. Pair-to-pair moves are still refused here as in the 8-bit section: the only register-to-register 16-bit load is into SP (ld sp,hl and the index-register twins). Everything else loads a constant (nn) or goes through a literal address ((nn)).

A 16-bit load fills both halves of a pair in one instruction. Reach for ld dd,nn whenever you need a pair to hold an address or a 16-bit count: a pointer to a table, the top of the stack, the length of something to copy. It is the setup line before almost every loop.

The driver parks the YM chip's DAC data port in DE before it starts streaming, so every sample can be written out through that one register:

        ld      de, SND_Z80_YM_A1       // de = $4001 (DAC data port; held all streaming)
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:353 (Aeon driver, .emp dialect)

One instruction drops the full constant $4001 into DE (D becomes $40, E becomes $01). It is loaded once and then used for the entire streaming loop, the payoff of a 16-bit load: set the address up front, spend nothing on it afterward.

The other 16-bit form reads a pair from memory. Here the driver loads a remaining-length word out of RAM into HL (the shadow HL', bracketed by the exx swaps that EXX explains):

        exx
        ld      hl, (SND_ROM_LEN)       // hl' = ROM len (shadow set)
        exx
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:360-362 (Aeon driver, .emp dialect)

The parentheses are doing the same job they did for a single byte: (SND_ROM_LEN) is the 16-bit value stored at that address, not the address itself. ld hl,(nn) reads two bytes from memory into the pair; ld hl,nn (no parentheses) would instead load the address as a literal number. The stack works in 16-bit pairs only, and the exact set is BC, DE, HL, AF, IX, IY. Two refusals catch newcomers: push sp does not exist (that encoding slot belongs to AF), and there is no push a or push b for a single 8-bit register. If you need one byte on the stack, push its pair.

PUSH qq stores a pair on the stack and steps SP down by 2; POP qq reads a pair back and steps SP up by 2. Reach for them to free a register you need but must hand back untouched, above all across a subroutine call that will clobber it. The shadow register set offers a faster alternative for whole-register-bank saves (see EXX), but the stack is the general tool.

The engine's sound interrupt handler is the textbook case. An interrupt can fire in the middle of anything, so on entry the handler pushes every pair it will disturb, does its work, and pops them back in exact mirror order, leaving the interrupted code none the wiser:

        push    af
        push    bc
        push    de
        push    hl
        call    Snd_PollMailbox_Banked
        pop     hl
        pop     de
        pop     bc
        pop     af
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:520-523, 528, 531-534 (Aeon driver, .emp dialect; the comment blocks between are left out)

The order is the whole lesson. The stack is last-in-first-out, so the pops must reverse the pushes: hl went on last, so it comes off first, and each pair lands back exactly where it started. The call in the middle is the reason all this is needed; it runs a routine that freely uses af, bc, de, and hl, and only the save-and-restore bracket keeps that from corrupting whatever the interrupt broke into. Every push stepped SP down, every pop stepped it back up, so SP ends where it began.

A smaller everyday case protects just one loop counter across a call that trashes it:

.fmloop:
        push    bc
        ld      de,zTrack.len                   ; Spacing between tracks
        add     ix,de                           ; Next track
        bit     7,(ix+zTrack.PlaybackControl)   ; Is it playing?
        call    nz,zFMUpdateTrack               ; If it is, go update it
        pop     bc
        djnz    .fmloop
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:468-475 (SMPS Z80 driver)

Here B counts the sound-effect channels, and djnz at the bottom decrements it and loops (see DJNZ on the sibling page). The trouble is that zFMUpdateTrack uses BC for its own purposes, so without protection the counter would be destroyed mid-loop. Bracketing the call with push bc and pop bc keeps the count intact for djnz. More driver pushes exist for this reason than for any other.

Gotcha · POP AF rewrites your flags

AF's low byte is the flag register, so pop af loads whatever 8 bits sit on the stack straight into S Z H P/V N C, wholesale. That is the feature and the trap. The feature: push af then pop af is the standard way to save and restore the flags across a routine that would otherwise clobber them. The trap: a stray pop af, or a push/pop pair that gets out of balance, silently overwrites your condition codes with data, and the next conditional branch reads a lie. Watch that every push af has its matching pop af.

A Zilog choice, not ours · no stack-relative addressing

The Z80 has no stack-relative addressing mode. Nothing lets you write ld a,(sp+2) and name a byte a fixed distance below the top. If you know the 68000, that machine has exactly that reach as d16(sp), and the Z80 simply does not. You can still get there, but you have to build the road yourself: ld ix,0 then add ix,sp copies the stack pointer into an index register, and from there (IX+d) reaches any slot you like (ex (sp),hl also swaps the top word in place, without popping). That is a frame pointer, hand-rolled, and it costs you an index register and a dozen T-states. Zilog gave you PUSH and POP and left the rest as an exercise. The consequence shapes Z80 code. Where a 68000 routine carves out a stack frame and keeps locals on it at fixed offsets, Z80 code keeps its working values in registers and in fixed RAM, and touches the stack only to stow a pair it will pop back soon. It is not a workspace; it is a coat-check. This is why the driver above pushes a register only to pop it a few instructions later, and never reaches "into" the stack.

4 · Exchanges

An exchange swaps two things at once, with no third register to hold one while the other moves. The Z80 has exactly five exchange shapes, no more (six instructions, counting the top-of-stack swap once each for HL, IX, and IY), and they split into two jobs: swapping two working locations (EX DE,HL, EX (SP),HL) and swapping in the second, hidden bank of registers (EXX, EX AF,AF'). The Programming Model page owns the shadow-set model in full; this section teaches the instructions and links back for the concept.

EX DE,HL · the workhorse

ex de,hl trades the two most useful pointer pairs in a single 4-T-state instruction, with nothing lost. Reach for it when the value you have built sits in one pair but the instruction you want next needs the other. The driver's modulation routine ends on exactly that bind. It popped its own return address into DE on the way in, and it has spent the routine building a frequency in HL; now it wants to hand that frequency back and jump home, and each of those wants the pair the other is holding:

        add     hl,bc                           ; Add modulation value
        ex      de,hl
        jp      (hl)                            ; WILL return to zUpdateTrack
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:1047-1049 (SMPS Z80 driver)

The add finishes the frequency in HL. One ex de,hl then does both halves of the job at once: the return address lands in HL, where the very next line, jp (hl), can jump to it (that computed jump lives on the sibling page), and the finished frequency lands in DE, which is where the caller collects it. That is what a swap buys you over a move: neither value is thrown away, so a single 4-T-state instruction delivers the result and sets up the return at the same time.

EXX and EX AF,AF' · the shadow set

The Z80 keeps a second copy of AF, BC, DE, and HL, written with a prime mark. You never name a primed register directly; the only thing you can do is swap a half of the shadow set in. EXX swaps the trio BC/DE/HL with their shadows and leaves AF alone; EX AF,AF' swaps AF and touches nothing else. The two halves swap independently, which is the point. The full model, including why the shadow set needs a clear ownership convention, is on The Programming Model, section 7.

The classic use is an interrupt handler grabbing a private set of working registers in one instruction instead of pushing three pairs. The Sonic 2 driver's interrupt entry does exactly that:

        push    af                      ; Save 'af'
        exx                             ; Effectively backs up 'bc', 'de', and 'hl'
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:397-398 (SMPS Z80 driver)

One exx hands the handler a fresh BC/DE/HL to work in, and a matching exx on the way out gives the interrupted code its registers back, with no stack traffic at all. (Note it still pushes AF separately: EXX does not touch AF, so the flags are saved the ordinary way.)

A subtler use keeps a value live in the shadow set for the length of a loop. Rather than a whole context switch, the driver parks a byte counter in the shadow HL' so the main HL stays free as a ring pointer, ducking into the shadow set only to tick the counter down:

        exx
        dec     hl                      // len-- (shadow)
        ld      a, h
        or      l
        exx
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:395-399 (Aeon driver, .emp dialect)

The first exx makes the shadow set live, so dec hl decrements the counter, not the ring pointer; ld a,h then or l is the 16-bit zero test (there is no single instruction for it, so you fold both halves through A; see DEC rr and OR); the second exx swaps the ring pointer back. Two swaps bracket the whole thing.

EX AF,AF' as a second accumulator

EX AF,AF' is not only for interrupts. The DAC decoder keeps its running total permanently in the shadow A', swapping it in to add each delta and swapping it back so the main A stays free for the raw sample byte:

        ex      af,af'                  ; 4     ; shadow register 'a' is the 'd' value for 'jman2050' encoding
.highnybble:
        add     a,(iy+0)                ; 19    ; Get byte from zDACDecodeTbl (self-modified to proper index)
        ld      (zYM2612_D0),a          ; 13    ; Write this byte to the DAC
        ex      af,af'                  ; 4     ; back to regular registers
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:695, 698-701 (SMPS Z80 driver)

The pattern is: swap in the running accumulator, add the table delta into it, emit the result, swap back. Between the two ex af,af' lines, A is the decoder's accumulator; outside them, A is the main one. The shadow A' holds the running value across every sample, a persistent second accumulator rather than a save/restore.

Gotcha · EXX leaves AF behind

It is easy to think EXX swaps "the registers" and expect the flags to come along. It does not: EXX swaps only BC/DE/HL, and AF stays put. To bank out everything, interrupt handlers run EX AF,AF' and EXX as a pair. If you rely on the shadow flags after a bare EXX, they are still the main ones.

5 · Arithmetic runs through A

The Z80's 8-bit arithmetic is built around one register. ADD, ADC, SUB, SBC, and CP all read one operand from the accumulator A, and all but CP write their result back into A. You do not add into B; you bring a value into A, operate, and take the result out. They are grouped as one entry because they share a shape; then INC and DEC, which work on any register and skip the carry flag, get their own.

The destination row is nearly all crosses on purpose: the result always goes to A (for CP it goes nowhere). You cannot add into B: there is no add b,c. On the source side, (BC) and (DE) are crossed because arithmetic reaches memory only through (HL) or (IX+d); add a,(bc) does not exist, even though ld a,(bc) does.

Two spelling conventions, one shape, which is what the syntax line above is showing. SUB and CP (and the logic operations) are written with a single operand because the A destination is implied: sub b means A ← A - B. ADD, ADC, and SBC spell that A out instead, as add a,b. All five read A, and all but CP write the result back into it; the spelling is the only thing that differs, and each mnemonic is still its own instruction with its own opcode.

New here? · why does everything pile up in A?

Picture an old desk calculator with a single running total on its display. You do not compute "3 plus 4" floating in the air; you key 3, press plus, key 4, and the display updates to 7. Every operation reads that one display and writes the answer back to it, so answers accumulate in the same place. On the Z80, A is that display. The chip has one 8-bit adder-and-comparer, and it is wired so that A is always one of its inputs and its output. That is the whole reason so much code has the rhythm "load a value into A, do arithmetic, read the result out of A," which is where the register gets its name, the accumulator.

Reach for these constantly: ADD and SUB for plain arithmetic and for stepping an index; ADC and SBC when a value is wider than a byte and a carry or borrow has to travel from the low byte into the high one; and CP to ask how A compares with something without disturbing it. CP is the star of the section, so it gets its own treatment below. First, the shared toy.

Pick an operation, set A and the operand, and run it. The accumulator and all six flags update; the note reads the result both ways where that matters. Watch CP leave A untouched while still setting every flag. Every logic and arithmetic entry on this page links here rather than growing its own flag readout.

Now the same instructions in the driver. SUB rebases a sound ID into a zero-based index (DAC samples are numbered from $81, so subtracting $81 gives 0, 1, 2, ...):

        sub     81h                     ; Subtract 81h (first DAC index is 81h)
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:509 (SMPS Z80 driver)

The consumer is the code just after it, which uses the rebased value in A as a table index. And ADD doubles a value as a cheap left shift: adding A to itself is a multiply by two, so two of them multiply by four:

        add     a,a
        add     a,a                     ; a *= 4 (each DAC entry is a pointer and length, 2+2)
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:513-514 (SMPS Z80 driver)

The comment names the payoff: each table entry is four bytes, so A * 4 turns the index into a byte offset into the table.

CP · compare is subtract-and-discard

CP s computes A - s, sets every flag exactly as SUB would, and then throws the difference away, leaving A untouched. That is what makes it useful: you learn how A relates to a value (equal, above, below) without spending A to find out. It never travels alone. A compare exists to be read by the branch on the very next line, so always show the two together:

        ld      a,(zAbsVar.QueueToPlay)
        cp      80h
        call    nz,zPlaySoundByIndex            ; If not 80h, we need to play something new!
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:427-429 (SMPS Z80 driver)

$80 is the "nothing queued" sentinel. The cp 80h sets the zero flag if the queued byte equals $80 and clears it otherwise; call nz then fires the play routine only when they differ. The compare set the note, the conditional call read it, and A still holds the queued byte afterward for whatever comes next. This two-beat rhythm, an operation that sets flags and a branch that reads one, is what the sibling page's jumps and calls all link back to.

New here? · how can comparing not change the number?

It feels like a compare must do something to A. It does not, and that is the whole point. CP runs the exact subtraction A minus the operand, watches how it came out (was it zero, did it borrow), writes those answers into the flags, and then discards the difference. A is left exactly as it was. So you can ask "is A equal to $80? is it below it?" as often as you like without ever spending the value in A. If you actually wanted A minus the operand kept, that is SUB; CP is SUB with the answer thrown away and only the flags kept.

New here? · after a subtract, why does carry mean "smaller"?

On an add, the carry flag is the bit that spilled off the top, a carry out. On a subtract the same flag is repurposed as a borrow: it lights when the subtraction had to borrow from beyond the top bit, which happens exactly when you take a larger number away from a smaller one. So after cp n or sub n, carry set means A was below n (both read as unsigned), and carry clear means A was greater than or equal. That is why jr c after a compare reads as "jump if below." It trips people because the word "carry" suggests going over a limit, while here it is really flagging going under one.

Gotcha · the sign of the carry after a subtract

After SUB, SBC, or CP, the carry flag means borrow: C = 1 says the subtraction went below zero, that is, A was smaller than the operand (read as unsigned). So cp n then jr c means "jump if A is below n." It is the opposite polarity from what "carry" suggests to a beginner, and mixing it up inverts the branch.

Unlike the arithmetic above, INC and DEC work on any 8-bit register, not just A, and on the memory byte at (HL) or (IX+d). This is a distinct 8-bit form; the 16-bit inc hl that steps a whole pair is a separate instruction with different rules, covered in section 6.

They add or subtract one, and reach for them wherever a value ticks by one: a loop counter, a timer, a position. The one thing to hold onto is what they do not touch. Here the driver counts a RAM timer down without loading it into a register at all:

        ld      hl,zPALUpdTick
        dec     (hl)
        jr      nz,.not_pal
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:445-447 (SMPS Z80 driver)

HL points at the timer; dec (hl) reads it, subtracts one, and writes it back in a single instruction; jr nz then loops while it is nonzero. The dec set the zero flag, and the very next branch consumed it. (The same pattern with inc (hl) appears at line 614, counting a delay tick up.)

A Zilog choice, not ours · INC and DEC preserve carry

The 8-bit INC and DEC set S, Z, H, and P/V, but they leave the carry flag exactly as it was. That is deliberate, and it is useful: it lets you set up a carry with one instruction, run a counter with DEC in the middle, and still have that carry intact for a multi-byte add or rotate afterward. The counter and the carry do not interfere. It is also a trap in the other direction: a wrap past 255 or below 0 never shows up in the carry, because the carry does not move at all. The other flags still report what happened, in their own way, so the wrap is not invisible: an inc that rolls $FF over to $00 sets Z, and P/V catches the signed overflow at $7F going to $80. But if what you actually need is a carry, to feed a multi-byte add or a jr c, INC and DEC will never hand you one. Use ADD or SUB for that.

6 · 16-bit arithmetic

The Z80 can add and subtract whole 16-bit pairs, but the family is smaller and quirkier than the 8-bit one, and the flags behave differently enough to trip people. There are three adds on HL with their own asymmetry, and then INC/DEC on a pair, which touch no flags at all and are the source of a classic bug.

Note the asymmetry the destination row is drawing. Plain ADD targets HL, IX, or IY; but ADC and SBC exist only with HL as destination, so adc ix,bc and sbc de,hl do not assemble. The source chips are coupled to the destination, which is the picky part: add hl,ss takes BC, DE, HL, or SP and never an index register, while add ix,pp takes BC, DE, IX, or SP and never HL. An index register can add itself but not the other one: add ix,ix assembles, add ix,iy does not. There is no 16-bit add against an immediate or a memory word; the source is always a register pair.

Reach for ADD HL,ss to fold a 16-bit offset into a running pointer, which is its everyday job. The driver's modulation code does exactly that, adding a computed offset into a frequency value:

        add     hl,bc                           ; Add modulation value

This is the line whose result EX DE,HL then shuffled into place for a computed jump. One instruction adds a full 16-bit pair into HL.

Gotcha · ADD HL cannot tell you if the result was zero

Plain ADD HL,ss sets H, N, and C, but it leaves S, Z, and P/V exactly as they were. So you cannot follow an add hl,de with jr z and expect it to test the 16-bit sum for zero; the zero flag never moved. When you need the full flags on a 16-bit result (a zero test, a sign, a signed comparison), use ADC HL,ss or SBC HL,ss, which do set them. The standard idiom is to clear the carry first with or a, then sbc hl,ss as a real 16-bit compare (see the driver excerpt below).

SBC HL,ss is the Z80's 16-bit compare. Because there is no cp for pairs, you clear the carry and subtract, then read the flags. The Sonic 2 driver does exactly that inside the Saxman decompressor that unpacks music data. A compressed stream can say "repeat something you have already written," and before the decoder can copy from that earlier position it has to check that the position is actually behind the write cursor:

        or      a                       ; Clear carry
        sbc     hl,bc                   ; hl -= bc
        jr      nc,.is_not_zero_fill    ; if result positive, jump ahead
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:4022-4024 (SMPS Z80 driver)

Both pairs hold positions in the output, measured from the same base: HL is how far the decompressor has written, BC is where the reference points. The or a is there because SBC subtracts the carry too, so you must know it is 0 before the subtract is trustworthy (see OR for why or a clears the carry without changing A). Then sbc hl,bc does the comparison, and the jr nc on the next line is the consumer: no borrow means HL was at least BC, so the reference lands inside data already written and the copy path takes over. A borrow (carry set) means the reference points past everything written so far, and the code that falls through fills that run with zeroes instead. The same subtract also sets Z, so a test for the two pairs being equal is jr z on the very same instruction, which is exactly what plain add hl could not give you.

New here? · why not just dec bc and jump if it hit zero?

Coming from the 8-bit world it looks obvious: decrement the counter, then jr nz to loop while it is nonzero. That works for dec b, because the 8-bit DEC sets the zero flag. But dec bc, the 16-bit version, is a different instruction that sets no flags at all, so there is nothing for jr nz to read; it would branch on whatever some earlier instruction happened to leave in the zero flag. To test a pair for zero you have to manufacture a flag yourself, and the standard trick is to OR its two bytes together (ld a,b then or c): the result is zero only if both halves were zero, so the zero flag then tells you the whole pair is zero. It is one of the small taxes the Z80 charges for being an 8-bit chip doing 16-bit work.

A Zilog choice, not ours · why the block instructions exist

The flag-silence of 16-bit DEC is exactly why the Z80 has a whole family of block instructions (LDIR, CPIR, and their kin, on the sibling page). Those fold "step the pointers, decrement the BC counter, and report whether the count ran out" into one instruction that does expose the counter through a flag. Where a plain dec bc loop needs the ld a,b / or c dance, a block instruction just repeats until BC is zero on its own. The silence here is the reason that family was worth building.

7 · Logic and masks

The bitwise operations work on A like the arithmetic ones, but instead of treating the byte as a number they treat it as eight independent bits. This is where the Z80's idiom culture lives thickest: xor a to zero a register, or a to test one, and to mask a status bit. Each idiom is a real instruction doing a job its plain name does not advertise.

Reach for AND to keep only certain bits (mask off the rest), OR to turn bits on or to fold several bytes together, and XOR to flip bits. For the flag lesson, all three run in the accumulator ALU above (pick and, or, or xor there); this entry shows the idioms. AND isolating a single status bit is the canonical mask, shown here in the engine driver with its consumer:

        ld      a, (SND_Z80_YM_A0)      // YM status: bit0 = Timer A overflow

The mask has exactly one bit set, the "Timer A overflowed" bit. The jp nz is the reaction, branching precisely when the bit survived. Masking to test one bit, then branching, is the everyday shape.

AND also extracts a field. After four rotates move a nibble into the low four bits (see the rotates on the sibling page), and 0Fh clears everything above it so A is a clean 0-to-15 index:

        and     0Fh                     ; 7     ; UPPER 4-bit offset into zDACDecodeTbl
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:693 (SMPS Z80 driver)

OR folds several "is anything set?" tests into one branch. Here the driver ORs three queue bytes together and calls one routine if any is nonzero:

        ld      a,(zAbsVar.Queue0)
        or      (ix+zVar.Queue1)
        or      (ix+zVar.Queue2)                ; This was missing in Sonic 1's driver, breaking the third queue slot.
        call    nz,zCycleQueue                  ; If any of those are non-zero, cycle queue
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:420-423 (SMPS Z80 driver)

After the two ors, A is zero only if all three bytes were zero, so call nz fires whenever any slot holds something. One branch, three conditions.

And XOR against yourself is the Z80's canonical "set A to zero":

        xor     a                       ; Clear 'a'
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:400 (SMPS Z80 driver)
New here? · why is xor a the way to write zero?

Exclusive-OR sets each result bit to 1 only where the two inputs differ. XOR a value with itself and every bit matches itself, so every result bit is 0: xor a always leaves A = 0, whatever it held before. It sounds like a roundabout way to write zero, and it is, but it is the smallest and fastest one: one byte and 4 T-states, against ld a, 0's two bytes and 7. It also sets the flags (Z becomes 1, carry becomes 0) as a bonus, which a plain load never does. Seeing xor a at the top of a routine, read it as "A = 0" and move on; it is so common it stops looking strange.

Cheaper cousins

xor a beats ld a, 0 for zeroing A (1 byte / 4 T vs 2 bytes / 7 T), and beats sub a only on clarity (both are 1 byte / 4 T; sub a also zeroes A, but xor a is the idiom everyone reads instantly). or a (or and a) beats cp 0 for testing A against zero on both counts: 1 byte and 4 T-states against cp 0's 2 bytes and 7, for the same Z and S. And cp 0 buys nothing on the carry either, which is the tempting reason to reach for it: A - 0 can never borrow, so cp 0 clears the carry exactly as or a does. or a before an ADC/SBC is the idiom precisely because that cleared carry is what those instructions need.

CPL replaces A with its one's complement: every 0 becomes 1 and every 1 becomes 0. Reach for it to invert a mask, or as the first half of a two's-complement negate (flip the bits, then add one; the one-instruction version of that is NEG). Its everyday job in the Sonic 2 driver is smaller and neater than either: a $00/$FF toggle, a stored byte that must come out the opposite of whatever it was last time. The bloop/gloop sound effect is only allowed to play every other time it is asked for, and CPL is the entire mechanism:

        ld      a,(zGloopFlag)
        cpl

zGloopFlag is one byte in the driver's own data area, starting at $00. The first request loads it, cpl flips it to $FF, and the store puts it back. The consumer is right there: or a tests the flipped value and ret z fires only when it came out zero, so this time A is $FF, the return is skipped, and jp zPlaySound plays the sound. The next request loads $FF, flips it to $00, and now ret z does fire and the sound is dropped. One instruction turns a byte into its own opposite, and the flag alternates forever. A few lines above, at s2.sounddriver.asm:2133, the same trick alternates zRingSpeaker between $00 and $FF, which is how consecutive rings pick opposite speakers.

8 · The decimal and carry corner

Four small instructions round out the page: one for decimal arithmetic, one for negation, and two that poke the carry flag directly. Only DAA is genuinely missing from the swept sound drivers (score and time code, where decimal math lives, are the 68000's job here), so it is the one entry on this page that teaches from a labeled worked example instead of real code. NEG, SCF, and CCF all appear in the swept drivers and the entries below quote the lines; CCF turns up exactly once in the whole Sonic 2 driver, and that one use is worth the visit.

DAA Decimal Adjust Accumulator daa

Flags Sresult Zresult Hresult P/Vparity Nunaff. Cresult S and Z from the adjusted A; P/V is parity; N is left alone (DAA reads it); H and C follow DAA's correction table
4 T-states the only form; 1 byte, no operand One form, one figure.
No operand grid: DAA works implicitly on A and takes no operand.

Binary-coded decimal packs one decimal digit into each nibble of a byte, so $25 stores the decimal number 25 (not the value $25 = 37). Ordinary ADD does not respect that packing: add BCD $25 and $18 and you get $3D, which is not a valid pair of decimal digits. DAA, run immediately after the add, corrects A back to $43, the BCD for 43. Reach for it only in decimal arithmetic (a score, a clock), and only right after an add-family or subtract-family operation on A.

New here? · what is binary-coded decimal?

Normally a byte holds a plain binary number from 0 to 255. Binary-coded decimal, or BCD, uses the same eight bits differently: it splits the byte into two 4-bit halves and stores one decimal digit (0 to 9) in each. So the byte $37 read as BCD means the decimal number 37, one digit per nibble, and the bit-patterns for $A through $F never appear. Why bother? A score or a clock is shown to a human as decimal digits, and BCD keeps each digit in its own nibble so you can read them straight out to the screen. The cost is that ordinary binary ADD does not know about the split and can leave an invalid half, which is exactly the gap DAA fills.

No sound-driver example exists, so here is a worked one:

Teaching example · not engine code 
        ld      a, 25h          ; BCD 25 (decimal twenty-five, not the value $25)
        add     a, 18h          ; binary add gives A = $3D, not a valid BCD pair
        daa                     ; correct it: A = $43, BCD for 43

The add produced $3D because the low nibble ran past 9 (5 + 8 = 13) with no decimal carry into the high nibble. DAA reads the half-carry and carry the add left behind, adds the right correction (here +6 to fix the low nibble and carry the ten into the high one), and leaves $43. It only works because the add set those flags first.

Gotcha · DAA only makes sense right after an add or subtract

DAA reads the N flag to know whether the last operation was an add or a subtract, and reads H and C to know which nibble overflowed. Run it after a logic operation (which leaves N = 0 and H per AND/OR) or a block instruction and it applies a meaningless correction. This is the one thing the otherwise-obscure N flag exists for: nothing else on the Z80 reads it.

DAA's correction, and the N flag

DAA chooses its correction from the current N, C, and H flags plus the two nibble values of A. After an add (N = 0) it adds one of +00, +06, +60, or +66; after a subtract (N = 1) it applies the symmetric -06, -60, or -66. It also updates C to reflect a decimal carry out of the high nibble, so chained multi-byte BCD arithmetic works. The exact per-row correction table is in UM0080 (pages 173-174); the shape above is faithful to it, but transcribe the precise added value from the manual if you implement DAA rather than just use it.

Source: UM0080. The undocumented flag bits follow the adjusted A; emulator detail only.

NEG Negate Accumulator (A ← 0 - A) neg

Flags Sresult Zresult Hresult P/Voverflow Nset Cresult behaves like SUB of A from 0: P/V is set only when A was $80, C is set for any nonzero A, N is set
8 T-states the only form; 2 bytes (ED-prefixed), no operand The two bytes are the ED prefix plus the opcode; there is no shorter encoding.
No operand grid: NEG negates A implicitly and takes no operand.

NEG replaces A with zero minus A, the two's complement negation: $01 becomes $FF (which reads as -1 signed), $05 becomes $FB (-5). Reach for it to flip the sign of a signed byte in A in one instruction, instead of cpl then inc a. The Sonic 2 driver uses it exactly once, and the job is vibrato. zDoModulation keeps a running modulation value, adds a small signed delta to it at a set interval, and adds the whole thing to the track's frequency, so the note's pitch drifts. Each sweep runs for a fixed number of steps, and when that count runs out the delta has to start pulling the other way:

        ld      a,(ix+zTrack.ModulationDelta)   ; get modulation change per mod step
        neg                                     ; flip it negative
        ld      (ix+zTrack.ModulationDelta),a   ; store negated value
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:1016-1018 (SMPS Z80 driver)

The delta comes out of the track's own state, neg turns +n into -n (or -n back into +n), and the store is the consumer: it puts the flipped value back where the next sweep will read it. What makes the negation meaningful is that the byte is treated as signed downstream. Further along the same routine the driver sign-extends this delta into BC and adds that to the running modulation value, so a negative delta walks the pitch down exactly as a positive one walked it up. Flipping the sign every time the step count expires is what makes the pitch wobble around the note instead of sliding away from it.

To see the flags for any input, run the sub path in the accumulator ALU with A = 0 and the operand set to your value: NEG is exactly 0 - A. The one edge case worth knowing is $80 (-128): its negation cannot fit in a signed byte, so NEG leaves $80 unchanged and sets the overflow flag P/V.

SCF / CCF Set Carry Flag / Complement Carry Flag scf · ccf

Flags (SCF) Sunaff. Zunaff. Hclear P/Vunaff. Nclear Cset SCF sets C, clears H and N, and leaves S, Z, P/V alone. CCF instead inverts C, and copies the OLD carry into H (see below)
4 T-states both; 1 byte each, no operand Neither has a variant, so neither has a variant's price.
No operand grid: both act on the carry flag alone and take no operand.

SCF sets the carry to 1; CCF flips it to its opposite. They are the carry knobs, for when you want to force a known carry before a rotate through carry (see the rotates on the sibling page) or an ADC/SBC, or to signal a routine's result back to its caller as the carry flag. That last job is SCF's everyday one. The engine's own Snd_DacLookup turns a DAC sample id into a pointer to that sample's descriptor, and its header comment states the contract: the carry comes back clear on success and set on a bad id. scf is how each of the two failure paths raises it:

        or      a
        scf
        ret     z                       // id 0 -> bad (carry set)
        cp      DAC_SAMPLE_COUNT+1      // carry SET iff id <= COUNT (valid)
        jr      c, .valid
        scf                             // id > COUNT -> bad (carry set)
        ret
aeon/engine/sound/z80_sound_driver.emp:741-747 (Aeon driver, .emp dialect)

Read the first three lines together and you can see why SCF can sit where it does. or a tests the id and sets Z if it is zero; scf then raises the carry without disturbing Z, because it moves only C, H, and N; so ret z on the next line still branches on the test from two instructions back and returns with the carry already up. The second failure path works the other way round: cp leaves the carry set when the id is in range, jr c takes those good ids away to .valid, so anything reaching the next line is out of range and gets scf then ret. The success path at .valid builds the pointer and finishes by clearing the carry with or a.

The consumer is the caller. Seq_HookDac is the sequencer's hook for a song's DAC trigger, and it decides whether to play anything at all by reading one flag:

        call    Snd_DacLookup           // a=id -> hl=descriptor (carry set if bad)
        ret     c                       // bad id -> ignore the trigger
        jp      Snd_StartSample         // start DAC playback from the descriptor
aeon/engine/sound/sound_sequencer.emp:1836-1838 (Aeon driver, .emp dialect)

ret c reads exactly the bit scf set and abandons the trigger; otherwise the code falls straight through to start the sample. The answer travelled from routine to caller in one flag, costing no register, and it survived the call boundary because RET touches no flags.

CCF covers the other case: you already have a carry and you want the opposite sense of it. The Sonic 2 driver flips one exactly once, in zBGMLoad, the routine that starts a new piece of music. The byte it has just fetched from the master playlist is not only a song number: its low five bits index the music table (and 1Fh strips the rest a few lines later) and its top three bits are flags, which the code peels off into the carry one at a time:

        add     a,a                             ; Adding a+a causes a possible overflow and a multiplication by 2
        add     a,a                             ; Now multiplied by 4 and another possible overflow
        ld      c,a                             ; Result -> 'c'
        ccf                                     ; Invert carry flag...
        sbc     a,a                             ; ... so that this sets a to FFh if bit 6 of original a was clear (allow PAL double-update), zero otherwise (do not allow PAL double-update)
        ld      (zAbsVar.IsPalFlag),a           ; Set IsPalFlag
s2disasm/s2.sounddriver.asm:1756-1761 (SMPS Z80 driver)

Each add a,a doubles A and pushes its top bit out into the carry, so after two of them the carry holds bit 6 of the original byte. The code wants the opposite of that bit, and ccf gets it in one instruction, with no branch and no spare register. Its consumer is the very next line. sbc a,a subtracts A from itself along with the carry, which leaves $00 when the carry was clear and $FF when it was set, smearing the flipped bit across a whole byte; the store then keeps that byte as the driver's PAL flag. Two instructions turn one flag into a mask, and CCF is what got the polarity right.

Gotcha · CCF does not clear H, it saves the old carry there

It is natural to assume CCF just flips the carry and zeroes the rest. It does clear N, but it copies the previous carry into the half-carry flag H rather than clearing H. Nothing you normally branch on reads H, so this rarely bites, but do not assume H is zero after CCF. Neither SCF nor CCF touches S, Z, or P/V. (The two undocumented bits after SCF/CCF are famously model-dependent across Z80 variants; no page should teach a specific value for them.)

Read the routine you started with. The Snd_DacLookup excerpt in the SCF entry is a whole procedure, and you can now read every instruction in it: or a tests the id for zero, scf raises the carry without touching the zero flag that test just set, ret z leaves on the id-zero case, cp puts the range check into the carry, and jr c takes the good ids away to the success path. Everything that sets a flag there is this page's; the two that jump on what those set, ret z and jr c, are the sibling page's, which is rather the point of splitting them. On the way in those were five unrelated mnemonics. Now they are one contract, "carry set means bad id", enforced in seven instructions and no registers, which the caller in the next excerpt reads with a single ret c.
